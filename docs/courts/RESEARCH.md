# Uganda Judiciary/Courts Research Brief

Research conducted to ground the design of "Pleadli Courts," part of Uganda's justice-system software family (Police, DPP, Courts, Lawyers, Prisons — see `CLAUDE.md`). Structured by the 7 question areas in the research brief, plus a References section. This is the first foundational research pass for the Courts side; it should be read alongside `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` (which it cross-references and, in one case — the Plea Bargain Rules — supersedes with newly-extracted primary text) and `docs/police/research/custody-and-bonds.md`.

**Confidence tags used throughout:** **[Primary]** = verified against the actual constitutional/statutory/official-document text fetched and read in full (or, for the one previously-unreadable PDF, visually read page-by-page after rasterizing it — see §4 and the References note on the Plea Bargain Rules). **[Secondary]** = drawn from a named secondary source (news, NGO, academic, or a government-adjacent but non-Judiciary page) because a primary source could not be located or did not establish the detail. **UNKNOWN** = not established by any source found; stated plainly rather than guessed.

**A methodological note on the Plea Bargain Rules PDF.** `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` reference #14 recorded that the Judiciary's own hosted copy of the Judicature (Plea Bargain) Rules, 2016 appeared to be an image scan with no extractable text layer, and that prior research pass could not read its substance. This research downloaded the same PDF and rendered each of its 19 pages to PNG with Ghostscript (`gs -sDEVICE=png16m`), then read the rendered images directly (no OCR tool was available in this environment — `tesseract`, `pdftoppm`, `pdfimages`, and `mutool` were all absent — so this was a direct human-style visual read of the scanned page images, not a text-extraction pipeline). This worked: the full substantive text of all 15 rules and all 3 Schedules was read and is reported below as **[Primary]**.

## 1. Court structure

**The constitutional hierarchy**, per Chapter Eight of the Constitution of the Republic of Uganda, 1995 (Articles 126–152; full chapter text fetched and read), Article 129(1): **[Primary]**

> "The judicial power of Uganda shall be exercised by the courts of judicature which shall consist of —
> (a) the Supreme Court of Uganda;
> (b) the Court of Appeal of Uganda;
> (c) the High Court of Uganda; and
> (d) such subordinate courts as Parliament may by law establish, including qadhis' courts for marriage, divorce, inheritance of property and guardianship, as may be prescribed by Parliament."

Article 129(2): the Supreme Court, Court of Appeal, and High Court "shall be superior courts of record." The subordinate courts referred to in 129(1)(d) are established by the Magistrates Courts Act (Chapter 19) — magistrates courts are the constitutionally-contemplated "subordinate courts," confirmed by that Act's own long title: "An Act to provide for the establishment, constitution and jurisdiction of, and the practice and procedure before magistrates courts." **[Primary]**

**Supreme Court** (Art. 130–133): consists of the Chief Justice plus not fewer than six other justices; duly constituted with an uneven number not less than five; is "the final court of appeal" (Art. 132(1)); hears appeals from Court of Appeal decisions, including the Court of Appeal sitting as the Constitutional Court. **[Primary]** Current serving strength per the Judiciary's own "Judicial Officers" page: 11 Supreme Court Justices. **[Primary — judiciary.go.ug]**

**Court of Appeal** (Art. 134–136): consists of the Deputy Chief Justice plus not fewer than seven justices of Appeal; duly constituted with an uneven number not less than three (five when sitting as the Constitutional Court, Art. 137(2)); hears appeals from High Court decisions. The Chief Justice may create divisions. **[Primary]** The Judicature (Amendment) Act, 2024 increased the number of Justices of Appeal from 15 to 35 (including the Deputy Chief Justice), per the Judiciary's own FY2023/24 Annual Performance Report. **[Primary — judiciary.go.ug-hosted report]**

**The Constitutional Court** (Art. 137) is not a separate court in the Art. 129(1) list — it is the Court of Appeal, sitting in a special constitutional-interpretation configuration of five members. **[Primary]**

**High Court** (Art. 138–141): consists of the Principal Judge plus judges of the High Court; has "unlimited original jurisdiction in all matters" (Art. 139(1)) and hears appeals from courts lower than itself. The High Court is organised into divisions; the Judiciary's own site lists seven: Civil, Criminal, Commercial, Land, Family, Anti-Corruption, and International Crimes. **[Primary — judiciary.go.ug "Administrative Structure" / "Judicial Structure" pages]** It sits at circuit stations around the country; per the FY2023/24 report, the Judicature (Designation of High Court Circuits) Instrument, 2023 (S.I. No. 94 of 2023) increased High Court Circuits from 20 to 38, and Parliament resolved to increase the approved structure of High Court Judges from 82 to 151 (including the Principal Judge). **[Primary — same report]** The Judiciary's own current-headcount page separately lists 67 sitting High Court Judges plus 21 Acting Judges (total 88) as of this research date — i.e., the 82→151 figure is an *approved establishment ceiling*, not yet the filled headcount; readers should not conflate the two. **[Primary, with an explicit note on what each number actually measures]**

**Magistrates courts** — the subordinate-court tier — are established under the Magistrates Courts Act. Per §4: **[Primary — full text fetched and read directly from a Laws.Africa/ULII-hosted consolidation, current to the 28 July 2023 Law Revision (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act]**

> "(2) Magistrates shall be of the following grades — (a) chief magistrate; (b) magistrate grade I; and (c) magistrate grade II. (3) The powers and jurisdiction of a magistrate shall be determined by the grade of his or her appointment..."

So **the Chief Magistrate / Magistrate Grade I / Magistrate Grade II distinction is real and current law**, not superseded terminology — confirming and sharpening the DPP research's incidental mention of it. §1(2)(a),(c) of the same Act also confirms these correspond to the older colonial-era "first class magistrate" / "second class magistrate" / "resident magistrate" (→ chief magistrate) terminology still found in older written law. A **government policy to phase out Magistrate Grade II** is corroborated independently: the Judiciary's own "Judicial Structure" page states the policy explicitly, and the Judiciary's current headcount page shows only **9** serving Magistrates Grade II nationally against 503 magistrates overall — i.e. the phase-out is empirically almost complete. **[Primary — judiciary.go.ug]**

**Where DPP-handled criminal trials actually happen, by level** (Magistrates Courts Act §161, "Criminal jurisdiction of magistrates," full text read): **[Primary]**

> "(1) Subject to this section, a magistrate's court presided over by — (a) a chief magistrate may try any offence other than an offence in respect of which the maximum penalty is death; (b) a magistrate grade I may try any offence other than an offence in respect of which the maximum penalty is death or imprisonment for life; and (c) a magistrate grade II may try any offence under, and shall have jurisdiction to administer and enforce any of the provisions of, any written law other than the offences and provisions specified in Schedule 2 to this Act."

And §162 ("Sentencing powers of magistrates"): a chief magistrate may pass any sentence authorised by law; a magistrate grade I is capped at 10 years' imprisonment or a fine not exceeding 240 currency points (or both); a magistrate grade II is capped at 3 years or 25 currency points (or both). **[Primary]** Capital offences (death penalty) and life-imprisonment offences are therefore reserved to the High Court by operation of the magistracy's jurisdictional ceiling, which is exactly why the Magistrates Courts Act's committal-for-trial mechanism (§168–169, already read and cited in `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §3 — reused here without re-fetching) exists: the DPP files an indictment and summary of the case, the magistrate commits the accused for trial by the High Court, and the matter physically moves up a tier. §164 ("Committal for sentence") is the analogous upward-referral mechanism when a magistrate convicts but believes a *heavier sentence* than their own ceiling is warranted. **[Primary]**

**Numbers.** The FY2023/24 Annual Performance Report states the Magistrates Courts (Magisterial Areas and Magistrates Courts) Instrument, 2024 (S.I. No. 11 of 2024) increased Magisterial Areas from 82 to 157 and Magistrates Courts from 396 to 696. **[Primary — judiciary.go.ug-hosted report]** The Judiciary's current-headcount page separately gives a serving-magistrate breakdown: Chief Magistrates 97, Senior Principal Magistrates 11, Principal Magistrates 23, Senior Magistrates 29, Magistrates (i.e. Grade I) 334, Magistrates Grade II 9 — total 503. **[Primary — judiciary.go.ug "Judicial Officers" page]** As above, the 396→696 figure is an approved-court-count target, not necessarily all currently staffed with a sitting magistrate; this research did not find a reconciliation between the two figures and does not attempt to guess one.

**Qadhis' courts** (Art. 129(1)(d)) are constitutionally contemplated for marriage/divorce/inheritance/guardianship matters "as may be prescribed by Parliament" — this research found no evidence Parliament has actually established these by statute; their operational existence is **UNKNOWN** and, being a civil/family jurisdiction in any case, is out of scope for Pleadli Courts' criminal-matters focus.

## 2. Core Judiciary record/terminology

**"Case" is the Judiciary's own dominant term**, not "cause" or "suit." This is not merely inferred from silence: the Magistrates Courts (Civil Procedure) Rules appended to the current Magistrates Courts Act consolidation (Rule 38, "Records," full text read) state: **[Primary]**

> "The following records shall be kept in all courts — (a) a file for each case containing particulars of the case, including the substance of the evidence given by the parties and their witnesses, a list of exhibits, if any, and judgment of the court; (b) a civil case register, which shall contain the following particulars — (i) the serial number of the case..."

A direct text search of both the Magistrates Courts Act and the Judicature Act (Chapter 13, full text of the "Arrangement of Sections" and most substantive Parts read) for the word **"cause"** as a defined technical term for the primary case record returned **nothing** — the word appears only in ordinary usage (e.g. "showing cause," in the civil-procedure debt-detention rules). **"Suit"** appears, but specifically in **civil** procedure contexts (the Judicature Act's Rules Committee provisions reference "regulating the procedure in suits by way of counterclaim," "regulating the consolidation of suits, appeals and other proceedings" — §41(2)(f)–(g)). **[Primary]** This research's own tentative conclusion: **"case" is the live, cross-cutting Judiciary term for a matter before any court (civil or criminal); "suit" survives as a civil-procedure-specific synonym; "cause" is not a distinct technical term in current Ugandan statute** for what Pleadli's domain model would call a case. This differs from ODPP's own usage (`docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §2), where "file" is the dominant *working* noun — the Judiciary's statutory language uses "case" where ODPP's operational Guidelines use "file"; both institutions use "case" as the umbrella term for the underlying matter, but ODPP's day-to-day paperwork noun is "file," not "case."

**One nuance found by reading a live official document, not a statute**: the Judiciary's own High Court cause lists (the weekly hearing schedules published per station, e.g. the Lira High Court Criminal Registry list for 22–26 June 2026, fetched and its extracted text read directly) are literally titled **"CAUSELIST."** **[Primary — judiciary.go.ug-hosted PDF]** So "cause list" (the scheduling document listing which matters are set down for hearing on which day) is genuinely live Judiciary terminology, even though the underlying matter itself is termed a "case," not a "cause," on that same document (the column header is literally "Case number"). Pleadli Courts should therefore distinguish a **case** (the matter/file) from a **cause list** (a scheduling artifact enumerating cases set for hearing in a date range) — these are not synonyms.

**Official court case-numbering convention.** No Act read (Constitution, Judicature Act, Magistrates Courts Act, Administration of the Judiciary Act) prescribes a case-numbering *format* in its text — numbering format is evidently left to Judiciary administrative/registry practice rather than being legislated. However, this research located and read a **live, current official document** that establishes the actual format in use for High Court criminal matters: a High Court (HCT) Criminal Registry causelist, fetched directly from judiciary.go.ug and its embedded text extracted and read. **[Primary]** The format observed, verbatim, across dozens of real entries:

> `HCT-10-CR-SC-0067-2025`, `HCT-10-CR-SC-0043-2022`, `HCT-10-CR-SC-0206-2021`, `HCT-10-CR-SC-0008-2020`, etc.

Decomposed: **HCT** (High Court of Uganda) – **{two-digit station/circuit code}** (e.g. `10` = Lira; other causelists found in search results use `00` for Kampala, `12` for Masindi, `25` for Kasese) – **CR** (Criminal) – **SC** – **{4-digit sequence}** – **{4-digit year}**. The meaning of the "SC" segment is **not independently confirmed against any primary Judiciary document read** — it most plausibly stands for "Session Case" (the historical Commonwealth term for a criminal trial before the High Court, "Criminal Sessions"), consistent with the "Criminal" case-category column on the same causelist, but this specific expansion is an informed inference, not a verified fact — treat it as **[Secondary/inferred]** pending a Judiciary registry-manual confirmation.

The same live causelist carries a **second, separate reference number** in its own "CRB No" column, e.g. `Lira/1260/2024`, `Aboke/04/2022`, `Dokolo/218/2021`, `Dokolo/599/2024`. **[Primary]** This is the Criminal Record Book number the Daily Monitor/UPF Deputy Director CID source described in `docs/police/research/custody-and-bonds.md` §5 (police-side SD→CRB conversion) — seeing it printed on an actual High Court cause list alongside the HCT case number **confirms, independently of that secondary source, that the CRB number is a real, currently-used reference that persists all the way to trial** and is tracked by the court registry as a cross-reference field distinct from the court's own case number. Its format — `{station or place name}/{sequence}/{year}` — is now directly evidenced, correcting the prior research's "not found" status on CRB format. **[Primary]**

**Magistrates-court-level case-numbering format** was **not found** in any primary source reached — this research did not locate a live Chief Magistrates Court or Magistrate Grade I causelist to confirm whether an analogous `{station-code}-CR-{sequence}-{year}` convention (without the `HCT`/`SC` High-Court-specific segments) is used, or something else entirely. **UNKNOWN** — do not assume the HCT format generalises downward without further verification.

## 3. The Judiciary's own case management system (ECCMIS)

**What it is.** The Electronic Court Case Management Information System (ECCMIS), per the Judiciary's own "About ECCMIS" page (fetched and read in full): **[Primary]**

> "a fully-featured system that automates & tracks all aspects of a case life cycle from initial filing through disposition & appeal."

It went live ("Go-Live") on 1 March 2022 per the Chief Justice's own announcement, corroborated in `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §5 and not re-verified independently here.

**What court staff can do**, per the same "About ECCMIS" page: paperless case management through the full lifecycle (mediation, case allocation, hearings, judgment delivery, execution, closure, archiving) with automated workflow and reporting. **[Primary]**

**What external parties can do — and this is the one place this research's findings *update* the DPP brief's more cautious conclusion.** The DPP research (`docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §5) fetched only the Judiciary's "About ECCMIS" overview page and found it made **no mention** of PROCAMIS (ODPP) integration, and treated any cross-institution data flow as unconfirmed aspiration. This research located and fetched a **different, more specific Judiciary-hosted page — "Benefits of ECCMIS"** (`judiciary.go.ug/data/smenu/144`) — which contains an explicit, direct primary-source statement the DPP research did not have access to: **[Primary]**

> "Data moves seamlessly from one justice sector institution to another, from Police to ODPP and to the Courts and to Prison Service, improving communication and reducing the likelihood of processing errors."

The same page also states that court staff get "access to integrated data from other institutions (DPP, Prisons, National ID)" within ECCMIS. **[Primary]**

**An important precision, so this isn't overread**: this statement describes data *conceptually* flowing along the justice chain (a case's information following it from Police → ODPP → Courts → Prisons) and court-staff-side lookup into that integrated data — it is **not** the same claim as "ODPP or Police staff have their own login/portal into ECCMIS." No source found in this research (nor in the DPP research) states that ODPP or Police institutionally hold direct system access *into* ECCMIS itself, as opposed to ECCMIS ingesting/displaying data that originated with them. Whether this integration is genuinely live and bidirectional in production today, or is itself the Judiciary's own aspirational description of a designed capability, is **not independently verifiable from the pages read** — treat "data moves seamlessly... from Police to ODPP... to the Courts... to Prison Service" as the Judiciary's own **stated design intent**, corroborated more specifically here than the DPP research could establish, but still short of independent operational proof.

**Advocates (defense counsel / lawyers) specifically do have a confirmed, real access tier**, and this is more concrete than the police/ODPP question above: **[Primary]**

- "Lawyers will be able to file case documents electronically, reducing the burden of paperwork on the court system." — About ECCMIS page.
- "a public portal for users to file documents electronically with access to information for all case types across all jurisdictions" — same page.
- Litigants can track case status via SMS/USSD, and receive SMS/email/in-system notifications for case registration, judicial-officer allocation, sittings, and judgment issuance.
- The Judiciary has run advocate-specific ECCMIS rollout/training events, e.g. a presentation at the 4th Annual Law Conference (26 August 2021) attended by the Principal Judge and Chief Registrar, at which "legal practitioners welcomed the initiative and pledged full support" while requesting stronger security/privacy safeguards, and news coverage of an "ECCMIS Rollout Gains Momentum with Advocates' Training" event and cluster-court "Practical ECCMIS Training for Justice Actors." **[Primary for the event occurring — judiciary.go.ug news pages fetched; Secondary/unconfirmed for exactly what capabilities advocates walk away with, since neither fetched article enumerates specific advocate-side functions beyond "electronic filing, payments, processes."]**

**Rollout status** (per the DPP research, not independently re-verified here): ECCMIS was rolled out to 31 courts in the Greater Kampala region as of the sources found, with a stated intention to expand nationwide — whether it now covers magistrates courts nationwide, or remains concentrated in High Court/Kampala-area stations, is **UNKNOWN** at this research's currency date.

**Net assessment for Pleadli's design purposes**: ODPP/Police do **not** have a confirmed, named external-user access tier into ECCMIS analogous to the advocate e-filing/tracking tier — the only concrete external-facing capability this research can confirm is for **advocates and litigants** (filing, tracking, notifications). Whether ODPP gets any equivalent tier is **UNKNOWN**; the "data moves seamlessly" language is real and Judiciary-authored, but describes institutional data flow along the case's lifecycle, not a confirmed ODPP/Police login capability into the Judiciary's own system.

## 4. Plea bargaining's institutional home

This section supersedes `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md`'s treatment of the Judicature (Plea Bargain) Rules, 2016 (Statutory Instrument No. 43 of 2016) with the actual rule text, read directly off the Judiciary's own hosted PDF after rasterizing its pages (see the methodological note at the top of this document). **All of the following is [Primary]** unless marked.

**Objectives** (Rule 3) — the Rules exist explicitly "to enhance the efficiency of the criminal justice system," "to facilitate reduction in case backlog and prison congestion," "to provide quick relief from the anxiety of criminal prosecution," "to encourage accused persons to own up to their criminal responsibility," and "to involve the victim in the adjudication process," alongside enabling the accused and prosecution "in consultation with the victim, to reach an amicable agreement on an appropriate punishment." This is a **Judiciary-authored statutory instrument** (made by the Rules Committee, chaired by the Chief Justice, under Judicature Act §41(1) and 41(2)(e) — a body that also includes the Attorney General, Deputy Chief Justice, Principal Judge, two Uganda Law Society-nominated advocates, and the Director of the Law Development Centre) — signed by Chief Justice Bart Magunda Katureebe as Chairperson of the Rules Committee. This is itself strong evidence that plea bargaining's *procedural framework* is a Judiciary institutional product, not a DPP-authored practice that courts merely rubber-stamp.

**Definition** (Rule 4): "'plea bargain' means the process between an accused person and the prosecution, in which the accused person agrees to plead guilty in exchange for an agreement by the prosecutor to drop one or more charges, reduce a charge to a less serious offense, or recommend a particular sentence **subject to approval by court**." The court's approval role is baked into the definition itself, not an afterthought.

**Initiation and scope** (Rules 5–6): may be initiated orally or in writing by **either the accused or the prosecution**, at any stage of proceedings before sentence is passed. Scope covers: a promise to plead guilty in exchange for a recommended lesser sentence; a promise to cooperate as a witness for the prosecution in exchange for reduced charges/sentence; or a guilty plea to a minor/cognate offence, a lesser offence, or some (not all) charges/counts in a multi-count case.

**Disclosure** (Rule 7): the prosecution must disclose "all relevant information, documents or other matters obtained during investigations" to the accused, in the interest of justice, subject to not compromising State security, witness security, or "the integrity of judicial process."

**Court participation — this is the clearest single answer to the brief's institutional-home question.** Rule 8, "Court participation in plea bargain":

> "(1) The court may participate in plea bargain discussions. (2) The parties shall inform court of the ongoing plea bargain negotiations and shall consult the court on its recommendations with regard to possible sentence before the agreement is brought to court for approval and recording. (3) Subject to sub rule (1), a judicial officer who has participated in a failed plea bargain negotiation may not preside over a trial in relation to the same case."

**The Judiciary's institutional role goes well beyond a single after-the-fact approval hearing**: the court may actively participate in the negotiation itself (not merely approve its output), the parties are rule-bound to keep the court informed *during* the negotiation, and there is a real conflict-of-interest safeguard (a judicial officer who sat in on a failed negotiation is disqualified from later presiding at trial on the same case) — implying the Rules anticipate plea-bargain discussions as a distinct judicial-officer-involving proceeding with its own record and consequences, not an informal DPP↔defence back-channel that only touches the court once at signature.

**Formal in-court procedure exists as a named, numbered sequence** — Schedule 2, "Procedure for Plea Bargain in Court" (rule 12(1)), an 18-step script: party called → representatives introduced → State introduces the Plea Bargain Agreement → Defence confirms it → court informs the accused of trial rights and the effect of a guilty plea → court confirms the accused signed voluntarily and understood a translated agreement → accused invited to execute a **confirmation** → agreement received onto the court record → charge read/explained → accused invited to plead → **plea recorded** → State summarises facts → if accepted as true, accused is "found guilty and convicted on his or her own plea of guilty" → State heard in aggravation → Defence heard in mitigation → convict heard in *allocutus* → victim/complainant's views on sentence heard → convict sentenced. This is a genuine, Judiciary-authored courtroom procedure, distinct from an ordinary guilty-plea colloquy, with its own schedule number and rule citation.

**Registry/documentary process**: Schedule 1 is the "Plea Bargain Agreement" form itself (filed *with a court and case number* — the form's header reads "IN THE ..... COURT OF UGANDA AT ..... UGANDA VERSUS ..... Case No ....."), containing biographical/case information (date of arrest, date of preferring charge, date of admission in prison, date of committal, date of grant of bail), a summary of agreed facts, aggravating/mitigating factors, the accused's plea and rights-waiver statements, a **Statement of Advocate**, a **Statement of Prosecuting Agent**, a **Statement of Court Interpreter/Translator** (where applicable), and a **Findings and Order of Court** section signed by the presiding Judge/Magistrate that formally orders "this form be filed and incorporated in the record of proceedings." Schedule 3 is a separate one-page "Plea Bargain Confirmation" form, also carrying its own Case No. field, signed by the accused in the presence of the accused's advocate and the State Attorney, before the Judge/Magistrate. **This directly answers the brief's "does DPP need its own copy, or is it Courts-owned" question for plea-bargain records specifically: the plea bargain agreement is filed into, and becomes part of, the court record — it is not a document that exists independently in ODPP's file and is merely referenced by the court.** ODPP's own Decision to Charge Guidelines (`docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §6) already stated ODPP keeps its own copy in the PROCAM file — so the correct model is: **the agreement is a Courts-of-record document by rule (formally filed, court-numbered, court-signed), and ODPP additionally and separately retains its own copy for its own case file** — both things are true simultaneously; this is not an either/or.

**Rejection, withdrawal, and protection** (Rules 13–15): the court **may reject** a plea bargain agreement "where it is satisfied that the agreement may occasion a miscarriage of justice," must record its reasons, and the rejected agreement becomes void and inadmissible in any subsequent trial on the same facts — the matter is then referred for ordinary trial. Either party may withdraw from the agreement at any stage before sentence. Statements made by the accused or their advocate during plea-bargain discussions are inadmissible for any purpose beyond resolving the case through a plea bargain (a genuine "without prejudice" protection). The court also cannot impose a sentence more severe than the agreement's recommended maximum (Rule — sentencing provision on the page following Rule 15, cross-referenced against the Constitution (Sentencing Guidelines for Courts of Judicature) (Practice) Directions, 2013) — reinforcing that sentencing discretion, while constrained by the agreement, remains a judicial act, not a rubber stamp of what the parties agreed.

**Is defence counsel's participation formally required, or can an unrepresented accused plea-bargain?** The Rules give a clear, direct answer: **representation is not an absolute precondition.** Rule 10, "Plea bargain agreement to be explained to accused person": "A plea bargain agreement shall, before being signed by the accused, be explained to the accused person **by his or her advocate or a justice of the peace** in a language that the accused understands..." The disjunctive "advocate **or** a justice of the peace" is the operative textual proof that an unrepresented accused can lawfully plea-bargain, provided a justice of the peace performs the explanation function an advocate would otherwise perform. This is independently corroborated by Schedule 1's own form content: it contains an explicit "RIGHT TO ADVOCATE AND WAIVER (WHERE APPLICABLE)" clause — "I understand that I have a right to be represented by an advocate of my choice throughout the proceedings... I hereby waive and give up my right to be represented by an advocate of my choice" — and Rule 12(1)(a)(vi)/Schedule 1's rights list explicitly includes "the right to be represented by an advocate of his or her choice at his or her expense **or**, in a case triable by the High Court, to legal representation at the expense of the State" as one of the rights the court must confirm the accused understands (and may waive) before accepting a plea. **So: representation is a right that can be waived, not a mandatory precondition for a valid plea bargain** — though the form's own "Statement of Advocate" section, and the fact that Schedule 1 defaults to assuming an advocate is present, suggests represented plea bargains are the practical norm the form was designed around, with the unrepresented path being the explicit but less-travelled alternative the Rules preserve.

**Judiciary-published plea-bargain statistics — real, current numbers.** The Judiciary's FY2023/24 Annual Performance Report (a document the Chief Justice is under a specific statutory duty to publish — see §7 below) treats the "Plea-Bargaining Programme" as a named, tracked institutional initiative, not an incidental byproduct of ordinary hearings: **[Primary]**

> "The number of cases disposed of through this initiative increased by 22% from 5,246 cases in FY 2022/23 to 6,408 cases in FY 2023/24."

A separate breakdown in the same report's "Innovations in Management of Cases" section states: 2,202 cases completed through day-to-day plea-bargain hearing at the various High Courts; 656 cases completed through Plea Bargaining Camps at the High Court level; 758 cases completed through Plea Bargaining Camps in the various Magisterial Areas (52.06% success/clearance rate across those camps); 3,550 cases completed at the Chief Magistrate Courts and 1,759 at Magistrate Grade I courts through day-to-day hearing. Per-venue camp performance is tabulated by name (e.g. Luzira Prison 73.08% success, Mukono High Court 89.06%, Lira High Court 18.29%, Lira Chief Magistrate 100.00%). **This research notes, without attempting to resolve, that these more granular figures (which sum to well over 6,408 across the venues listed) do not obviously reconcile with the single national 5,246→6,408 headline figure quoted above — the report does not state whether the 6,408 figure is a specific subset (e.g. only "Programme"-labelled disposals, excluding ordinary day-to-day guilty pleas that happen informally to be negotiated) or the two figures use different counting boundaries. Flagged as an open reporting-methodology question rather than silently reconciled.**

**A further institutional-ownership signal**: the report's own photo captions describe "Chief Justice opens a Plea Bargaining session at Luzira Upper Maximum Security Prison" — i.e., the Chief Justice personally inaugurates plea-bargaining camps as a matter of institutional ceremony/leadership, which is a further concrete data point (beyond the Rules Committee's authorship of the governing Rules) that the Judiciary treats the plea-bargaining *programme* as its own initiative, not merely a legal mechanism it happens to approve when DPP and defence bring one to it.

**Bottom line for the brief's core question**: plea bargaining is **genuinely cross-institutional by design**, but with a real, non-trivial, rule-defined Judiciary institutional role that goes past "approve in open court" — the court may participate in negotiations, has a named conflict-of-interest rule protecting trial impartiality, runs its own numbered in-court procedure (Schedule 2), owns the filed agreement and confirmation as part of its own court record (Schedules 1 and 3, both case-numbered), can reject or cap the agreement's sentencing terms, and — as an institution — runs and publicly reports on plea-bargaining "camps" as a named backlog-reduction programme that the Chief Justice personally opens. DPP's role (negotiation, victim consultation, disclosure, drafting the agreement, its own Plea Bargain Unit per `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §7) is real and substantial, but it is not accurate to model plea bargaining as "a DPP process that only touches Courts at approval" — the Rules themselves place the court inside the negotiation loop, not just at its end.

## 5. What DPP legitimately needs vs. what stays Courts-only

This research looked specifically for a Judiciary-authored (not DPP-authored) statement on this question, per the brief's instruction. **None was found.** No Judiciary Act, Rule, practice direction, or judiciary.go.ug page fetched in this research states an explicit position on what external justice-sector institutions may or should track locally about a case versus what remains purely internal Judiciary record-keeping. **UNKNOWN.**

What this research *can* offer, short of that explicit statement, is the pattern of evidence assembled above, which points toward a workable design answer even without a canonical Judiciary policy text:

- **The plea bargain agreement itself is unambiguously a Courts-of-record document** (case-numbered, court-filed, judicially signed and incorporated into "the record of proceedings" per Schedule 1's own closing clause) — see §4. A Pleadli Courts / Pleadli DPP data model should treat the *authoritative* plea bargain agreement as Courts-owned, with DPP's own PROCAM-file copy being exactly that: a copy, for DPP's own operational tracking, not a competing source of truth.
- **"Court Matters" fields that ODPP's own Decision to Charge Guidelines already say it tracks** (`docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §5 — court case number, committal status, trial-date notice, adjournment count, bail status, plea/case-progress status) are, per this research, all things that also live natively inside the Judiciary's own case record and (per ECCMIS's stated design, §3 above) are meant to flow from Courts outward to other institutions, not the reverse. This is consistent with a "Courts is authoritative, DPP keeps an operationally-necessary local copy/summary" model rather than "DPP is authoritative and Courts merely receives DPP's filings."
- **No evidence was found of the reverse** — i.e., no source suggests the Judiciary keeps its own copy of ODPP-side data (charge-sanctioning file contents, ODPP registration numbers, internal review reasons) beyond what is formally filed with it (the Charge Sheet, indictment, summary of the case, and — where applicable — the plea bargain agreement). This is consistent with each institution being authoritative for its own procedural artifacts, while both legitimately reference the other's case-identifying numbers (the "at minimum three distinct reference numbers in play on one matter" already identified in `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §2: police reference, ODPP registration number, court case number — this research adds a fourth in practice, the CRB number, which persists visibly onto the High Court's own cause list; see §2 above).

**This research's honest conclusion**: there is no Judiciary-published policy statement settling the "DPP's own copy vs. Courts-only" question in the abstract, so a Pleadli product-design decision here would be a **design choice informed by the pattern of evidence**, not a codified rule this research can cite. The pattern strongly supports: Courts owns the authoritative record of court-facing facts (case number, hearing dates, adjournments, judgment/sentence outcomes, the plea bargain agreement once filed); DPP legitimately and necessarily keeps its own working copy of the subset of those facts it needs operationally (as its own Guidelines already describe), but that copy is not, and should not be modelled as, an alternative source of truth.

## 6. Roles that matter

**Statutory basis for the category "judicial officer."** The Administration of the Judiciary Act, 2020 (an Act to "give effect to Chapter Eight of the Constitution relating to the Judiciary," assented to by the President on 19 June 2020 — full text of its Interpretation section and Parts II–V fetched via the same page-rasterizing method used for the Plea Bargain Rules, since this PDF was also an image scan) defines, in §1: **[Primary]**

> "'judicial officer' means the Chief Justice, the Deputy Chief Justice, the Principal Judge, a Justice of the Supreme Court, a Justice of the Court of Appeal, a Judge of the High Court, **the Chief Registrar, a Registrar of a Court, a Magistrate** or such other person holding any office connected with court as may be prescribed by law."

This is an important, concrete finding: **Registrars and Magistrates are statutorily "judicial officers,"** on the same defined footing as judges — not merely administrative staff who happen to work in a courthouse. This matters for a Pleadli Courts permissions/roles model: a "Registrar" role should be modeled as a judicial-officer-class user, not a clerical one.

**Chief Registrar** — constitutional basis: Article 145 of the Constitution ("Registrars"): "(1) There shall be in the judiciary the office of Chief Registrar and such number of registrars as Parliament may by law prescribe. (2) The Chief Registrar and a registrar shall be appointed by the President on the advice of the Judicial Service Commission." **[Primary]** Statutory detail, Administration of the Judiciary Act §15: a person cannot be appointed Chief Registrar or Registrar under Art. 145(2) unless eligible for appointment as a Judge of the High Court (i.e. senior legal qualification is required). The Chief Registrar is responsible for: performing judicial functions vested in the office by law; giving effect to Chief Justice/Deputy Chief Justice/Principal Judge policies and directions; **effectively overseeing judicial operations of all the courts of judicature**; monitoring and enhancing service quality; communicating with Government/public on Judiciary matters; implementing the Judiciary Strategic Plan; assisting the Chief Justice, Deputy Chief Justice, and Principal Judge in facilitating/supervising the courts; **linking the Judiciary and Judicial Service Commission on appointments, promotions and disciplinary matters relating to Registrars and Magistrates**; and any other Chief-Justice-assigned matter. The Chief Registrar reports to the Chief Justice. **[Primary]**

**Registrars (plural, §16)**: there shall be a Registrar responsible for (a) the registry at (i) the Supreme Court, (ii) the Court of Appeal, and (iii) the High Court; (b) planning, research and development; (c) human resource development and training; (d) any other Chief-Justice/Chief-Registrar-assigned matter. **[Primary]** This matches, and gives statutory grounding to, the Judiciary's own "Administrative Structure" web page description of named specialist registrars (Registrar of the Supreme Court, Registrar of the Court of Appeal, Registrar of the High Court, Registrar for Research and Training, Registrar for Planning and Development, an Inspector of Courts) plus Deputy and Assistant Registrars. **[Primary — judiciary.go.ug]** Current headcount, per the Judiciary's own site: 1 Chief Registrar, 10 Registrars, 43 Deputy Registrars, 24 Assistant Registrars (78 total). **[Primary]**

**Registrars perform real judicial functions**, not merely administration — corroborated by two independent primary/near-primary sources: (1) the Judicature Act §41(2)(c) empowers the Rules Committee to make rules "prescribing what part of the business which may be transacted or the jurisdiction which may be exercised by judges of the High Court may be transacted or exercised by registrars or other officers of the court" — i.e. Registrars can be delegated a slice of a judge's own business by court rule; **[Primary]** and (2) a web search corroborates Registrars hearing urgent/interim-order applications in practice. **[Secondary — search-summarized, not independently fetched and read in full]**

**Every magistrate's court has its own Chief Registrar/Registrar too** — this is a separate, lower-tier office from the Supreme Court/Court of Appeal/High Court Registrars above. Magistrates Courts Act §8, "Chief registrar and registrar" (full text read): **[Primary]**

> "(1) There shall be appointed for every magistrate's court a fit and proper person to be or to act as chief registrar or registrar of that court. (2) The chief registrar or registrar shall, subject to the general supervision and control of the Chief Justice, be under the immediate control and direction of the magistrate presiding over the court to which he or she is assigned. (3) The offices of chief registrar and registrar of a magistrate's court shall be offices to which Article 147(3)(b) of the Constitution applies."

(This research did not independently fetch and read Article 147(3)(b) itself to confirm what protection it affords these offices — flagged as **unread cross-reference**, not verified.)

**Deputy Chief Justice**: head of the Court of Appeal and (per Art. 137) the Constitutional Court; deputises for the Chief Justice; assists the Chief Justice in administering the Court of Appeal (Art. 136). **[Primary]**

**Principal Judge**: "head of the High Court," assists the Chief Justice in administering the High Court **and subordinate courts** (Art. 141(1)(a) — note this explicitly extends the Principal Judge's administrative remit down into the magistracy, not just the High Court itself). **[Primary]**

**Secretary to the Judiciary** (Administration of the Judiciary Act §17): appointed by the President under Article 174 of the Constitution; responsible for the organisation of the Judiciary, tendering advice to the Chief Justice on administrative business, implementing government policy and the Judiciary Strategic Plan's administrative activities, and — subject to Art. 164 — expenditure of public funds by/for the Judiciary; is answerable to Parliament (a distinct accountability channel from the rest of the judicial hierarchy). **[Primary]**

**Chief Inspector of Courts / Inspectorate of Courts** (Administration of the Judiciary Act Part III, §8–9): a Supreme Court Justice designated by the Chief Justice, holding office for a renewable 3-year term, reporting to the Chief Justice; functions include receiving/processing internal and **external** complaints against Judiciary staff, investigating maladministration of justice, examining and taking custody of judicial/administrative records for investigations, recommending remedial action, and enforcing the Judicial Code of Conduct. **[Primary]** This is a real, named accountability/complaints channel that a Pleadli Courts product touching complaints-handling should be aware exists institutionally, separate from ordinary appeal.

**Judiciary Council** (Administration of the Judiciary Act Part II, §4–7): advises the Chief Justice on Judiciary/justice administration; has sub-committees on (a) planning/development/finance, (b) human capital/development/gender/equity mainstreaming, (c) information/communications technology/documentation, plus any others the Chief Justice creates. **[Primary]** Not directly case-workflow-relevant, but relevant to understanding who sets Judiciary-wide policy (e.g. an ICT committee is presumably the internal governance body behind ECCMIS's rollout, though this specific link is **UNKNOWN** — not stated explicitly in any source read).

**Court Clerk**: no statutory definition of this specific title was found in any Act read (Constitution, Judicature Act, Magistrates Courts Act, Administration of the Judiciary Act all use "officers of courts"/"chief registrar, registrars, deputy registrars and assistant registrars" as their defined categories — Judicature Act §43 — rather than naming "Court Clerk" as a distinct statutory office). A web search corroborates a real, practice-level role: Court Clerks "register cases brought to Court," assist the Judge/Magistrate in the courtroom (reading court files), and interpret for parties who do not speak English. **[Secondary — search-summarized description, not independently fetched from a primary Judiciary HR/establishment document]** Treat "Court Clerk" as a genuine operational role (likely falling under the Judicature Act's "officers of the courts... necessary for the performance of any special duties in connection with the business of the courts," §43(1)) rather than a title carrying its own constitutional or Administration-of-the-Judiciary-Act definition.

**Rank/authority summary relevant to a Pleadli Courts permissions model** (synthesised from the above, all [Primary] except Court Clerk):

| Role | Appointed by | Judicial officer? | Core function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chief Justice | President, on JSC advice, Parliament approval | Yes | Head of Judiciary; administers/supervises all courts; issues binding orders/directions |
| Deputy Chief Justice | as above | Yes | Head of Court of Appeal/Constitutional Court; deputises for CJ |
| Principal Judge | as above | Yes | Head of High Court **and subordinate courts** administratively |
| Justice (SC) / Justice of Appeal / Judge (HC) | as above | Yes | Adjudicate at their respective tier |
| Chief Registrar | President, on JSC advice | Yes | Oversees judicial operations of all courts; JSC liaison on Registrar/Magistrate appointments/discipline; some delegated judicial functions |
| Registrar (SC/CoA/HC, or of a magistrate's court) | President (superior-court Registrars) / appointed per §8 MCA (magistrates-court Registrars) | Yes | Runs a specific court's registry; delegated judicial business per Rules Committee rules; magistrate's-court Registrar works under that magistrate's immediate direction |
| Chief Magistrate / Magistrate Grade I / Magistrate Grade II | Judicial Service Commission process (Minister consults CJ on numbers needed, MCA §4) | Yes | Try criminal cases up to their grade's jurisdictional/sentencing ceiling |

| Chief Inspector of Courts | Chief Justice, from among Supreme Court Justices | Yes (as a sitting Justice) | Complaints, maladministration investigation, Code of Conduct enforcement |
| Court Clerk | UNKNOWN (not found in statute) | UNKNOWN — likely not, per absence from the statutory list | Registers cases, courtroom support, interpretation (per secondary source only) |

## 7. Service standards / case backlog statistics the Judiciary itself publishes

**The publication duty is itself statutory**, which matters for citing these figures with confidence: Administration of the Judiciary Act §39, "Annual report of the Judiciary" (full text read): **[Primary]**

> "(1) The Chief Justice shall publish an annual performance report concerning all activities of the Judiciary during the financial year. (2) The report shall include information on the state of the Judiciary in the country. (3) The Chief Justice shall cause to be distributed, copies of the annual performance report to the President, the Speaker, and any other stakeholder."

The document this research drew statistics from — the **"Strengthening Administration of Justice: The Judiciary" Annual Performance Report, FY 2023/24** — is exactly this statutory report (its own text cites "Section 39(1) of the Administration of the Judiciary Act" as its basis), fetched directly from judiciary.go.ug and its full text extracted and read. **[Primary]**

**Headline caseload/disposal figures, FY2023/24**:

- Total caseload: **401,269** cases (143,205 brought forward from FY2022/23 + 258,064 freshly registered in FY2023/24).
- Completed: **239,431** cases — an overall disposal rate of **59.67%**.
- Average time from filing to disposal: **891 days (≈2.5 years)** in FY2023/24, down from **1,074 days (≈3 years)** in FY2021/22.
- Public confidence in courts (an external survey figure, not the Judiciary's own measurement): **71%**, per a Legal Aid Service Providers' Network (LASPNET) survey cited in the report. **[Secondary, as reported inside a Primary document — the underlying survey itself is LASPNET's, not the Judiciary's own measurement]**

**Backlog** (a case is defined by the Judiciary as backlog "if it has spent two or more years in the court system before it is disposed of or completed" — this is itself a useful, citable operational definition for a Pleadli Courts backlog-flagging feature): pending caseload at year-end **161,838**, of which **42,588 (26.32%)** were backlog. Backlog declined by **0.87% (372 cases)** from 27.48% (sic, likely 27.19% pending-caseload-percentage terms; quoted as printed) in FY2022/23.

**Named backlog-reduction initiatives, with their own reported figures**:
- **Plea-Bargaining Programme**: 5,246 → 6,408 cases (+22%), FY2022/23 → FY2023/24 (see §4 above for the fuller breakdown and the reconciliation caveat).
- **Small Claims Procedure (SCP)**: rolled out to 203 courts; 23,567 cases completed, recovering claims totalling UGX 17,993,850,733.
- **Mediation**: 513 of a 1,274 caseload mediated to conclusion.

**Structural capacity investments reported for FY2023/24** (Justice count/infrastructure, not case-flow, but relevant "service standard" context): Judicature (Amendment) Act, 2024 raised Court of Appeal Justices from 15 to 35 (incl. Deputy Chief Justice); Parliament resolved to raise the High Court Judges structure from 82 to 151 (incl. Principal Judge); High Court Circuits raised from 20 to 38 (S.I. No. 94 of 2023); Magisterial Areas raised from 82 to 157 and Magistrates Courts from 396 to 696 (S.I. No. 11 of 2024); new Supreme Court and Court of Appeal buildings were completed and commissioned by the President in FY2023/24, ending both courts' prior existence in rented premises. **[Primary — all from the same Annual Performance Report]**

**Budget** (per the DPP research's earlier finding, not independently re-verified here but consistent with this report's own framing): Judiciary budget rose from UGX 383.25bn (FY2022/23) to UGX 392.54bn (FY2023/24). **[Secondary, as previously reported — not independently re-confirmed against the primary report text in this pass]**

**Strategic framework**: the report frames its own performance analysis against the **Judiciary Strategic Plan V, FY 2020/21–FY 2024/25** — i.e. there is a live, named, multi-year strategic plan the annual report is measured against, though this research did not fetch and read the Strategic Plan V document itself (only the annual report's references to it). **UNKNOWN in detail; confirmed to exist by name.**

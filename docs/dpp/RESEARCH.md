# Uganda ODPP Research Brief

Research conducted to ground the design of "Pleadli DPP," a purpose-built desktop app for Uganda's Office of the Director of Public Prosecutions (ODPP), sibling to Pleadli Police. Structured by the 8 question areas in the research brief, plus a References section.

**Confidence tags used throughout:** **[Primary]** = verified against the actual constitutional/statutory/official-document text fetched and read in full during this research. **[Secondary]** = drawn from a named secondary source (news, NGO, academic, or non-ODPP government page) because a primary source could not be located or did not establish the detail. **UNKNOWN** = not established by any source found; stated plainly rather than guessed.

---

## 1. Institutional structure & legal mandate

**Constitutional mandate.** Article 120 of the Constitution of the Republic of Uganda, 1995, establishes the Office of the Director of Public Prosecutions. Its functions, per Article 120(3), are: **[Primary]**

- (a) to direct the police to investigate any information of a criminal nature and to report to the DPP expeditiously;
- (b) to institute criminal proceedings against any person or authority in any court with competent jurisdiction other than a court-martial;
- (c) to take over and continue any criminal proceedings instituted by any other person or authority;
- (d) to discontinue, at any stage before judgment, any criminal proceedings to which the article relates, whether instituted by the DPP or any other person or authority — **except** that the DPP shall not discontinue proceedings commenced by another person or authority without the consent of the court.

This exact text (with section numbering (a)-(d)) is quoted verbatim in ODPP's own 2023 "Guidelines on the Decision to Charge," §1.3.1, which I fetched and read in full — so the wording above is confirmed against an ODPP primary document, not just a paraphrase. **[Primary]**

**Delegation.** Article 120(4): functions under 120(3)(a), (b) and (c) may be exercised by the DPP in person or by officers the DPP authorises by general or specific instructions. The function to **discontinue** proceedings (120(3)(d)) has no such delegation clause — it is exercised by the DPP personally. ODPP's own Guidelines state this explicitly: "the mandate to withdraw charges is exercised exclusively by the Director of Public Prosecutions (DPP)... all other functions of the DPP enshrined in Article 120 are exercised by the DPP or officers authorised by the DPP." **[Primary]**

**Independence and guiding principles.** Article 120(5): in exercising these powers the DPP "shall have regard to the public interest, the interest of the administration of justice and the need to prevent abuse of legal process." Article 120(6): in exercising the Article 120(3) functions, the DPP "shall not be subject to the direction or control of any person or authority." **[Primary]**

**Appointment.** The DPP is appointed by the President on the recommendation of the Public Service Commission and with the approval of Parliament, and must be qualified to be appointed a judge of the High Court. **[Primary — confirmed via ODPP's own "About" page at dpp.go.ug, consistent with search-derived paraphrase of Art. 120(1)-(2); I could not fetch the raw constitutional text of 120(1)-(2) directly due to ulii.org blocking automated fetches, so this specific clause is corroborated by ODPP's own site rather than the constitutional text itself.]**

**"ODPP Act 2019" — could not be verified.** The research brief asked me to verify whether an "ODPP Act 2019" established ODPP as an independent institution with its own Service Commission. Despite searching ODPP's own site, ULII's 2019 Acts, Uganda Parliament's bills listing, and general web search, **I found no evidence that such an Act exists.** ODPP's own "About" page cites only Article 120 of the Constitution as its legal foundation, and describes the DPP's appointment as going through the general Public Service Commission (not an ODPP-specific Service Commission). **UNKNOWN / likely does not exist as described** — this should be treated as an open question for a domain expert rather than assumed true. If a 2019 Act does exist, it was not discoverable through the primary-source channels available to this research (odpp.go.ug, ulii.org's Acts index, parliament.go.ug).

**Internal organisational structure.** Fetched directly from ODPP's own site (dpp.go.ug/directorate/ and dpp.go.ug/top-management/, current as of this research). **[Primary]** ODPP is organised into **four directorates**, each headed by a Deputy Director of Public Prosecutions (DDPP):

1. **Directorate of Prosecutions** — Anti-Corruption Department; Department of Gender, Children and Sexual Offences; Land Crimes Department; Department of General Case Work (with a Homicide Division, a Building Control Crimes Division, a Cybercrime Unit, and a Plea Bargain Unit); Department of Appeals and Applications.
2. **Directorate of International Affairs** — International Crimes Department (with an Anti-Trafficking in Persons Division); International Cooperation and Asset Recovery Department (with an Asset Recovery Division).
3. **Directorate of Inspections, Quality Assurance, Research and Training** — Department of Inspections and Quality Assurance; Department of Research and Training.
4. **Directorate of Management Support Services** — Department of Field Operations (with Human Rights and Environment/Wildlife Divisions, and **19 Regional Offices** covering **127 Resident State Attorney/Prosecutor stations**); Department of Witness Protection and Victim Empowerment; Finance and Administration Department; Department of Information and Communication Technology.

**Rank hierarchy** (descending), per the same source: Director of Public Prosecutions (DPP) → Deputy DPP (DDPP) → Principal Assistant DPP → Assistant DPP (ADPP) → Chief State Attorney (CSA) → Senior State Attorney → State Attorney → Resident State Attorney (RSA) / Resident State Prosecutor (RSP). **[Primary]**

**How work is assigned/supervised.** The Decision to Charge Guidelines (ODPP's own document, 2023) give the clearest primary-source picture of supervision in practice: a decision to charge (or not to charge) is made by "the action officer (Prosecutor)"; reviews "can be conducted by any Prosecutor seized of/assigned the matter"; supervisors and a "performance management team" ensure regular reviews for quality control "as a matter of practice"; a Prosecutor must "consult with a supervisor before conducting a case that is beyond their experience"; and conflicts of interest must be disclosed "to their supervisor." Prosecutors record decisions "in sufficient detail on PROCAM and/or PROCAMIS" (see §2 below). **[Primary]** Beyond this, no ODPP document I found specifies a formal, codified case-assignment algorithm (e.g., how a file is routed to a specific Resident State Attorney) — that operational detail is **UNKNOWN**.

## 2. Core record terminology

ODPP's own Decision to Charge Guidelines are unusually precise about terminology, since they define terms formally. **[Primary]**

- **"File"** is the dominant working term inside ODPP for the primary unit of work — e.g., "every single file that is presented to them for a decision to charge," "the investigation file," "case files containing charges that are not linked... will need to be separated, each with a different ODPP registration number."
- **"Case"** is used more loosely/generally (e.g., "cases involving multiple offences," "the case," "ongoing prosecutions") but is not defined as a distinct technical term from "file."
- ODPP maintains its **own file, separate from the police file**: "A copy shall be retained in the **ODPP PROCAM file**... The ODPP file itself should remain with the Prosecutor and under no circumstances may a file be left unattended in the court or office." This is an explicit, primary-source confirmation that the police file and the ODPP's own file are distinct artifacts, and that the ODPP file has continued custodial significance even after the charge sheet is filed in court.
- **File numbering**: ODPP assigns its own **"ODPP registration number"** to a file. The Guidelines state that charges not founded on the same facts/same character "need to be separated, each with a different ODPP registration number" — confirming (a) this number exists, and (b) one number can cover multiple joined charges/counts but not unrelated ones. The exact format/pattern of this number was **not found** in any source I could fetch — **UNKNOWN**.
- Separately, a **court case number** is assigned when the Charge Sheet is filed in the court registry ("a court file is opened, and a case number assigned — this number must be recorded on the ODPP's copy of the Charge Sheet as well as the ODPP file"). So there are at minimum **three distinct reference numbers** in play on one matter: a police-side reference, the ODPP registration number, and the court case number.
- **PROCAM** and **PROCAMIS** are named ODPP information systems: "PROCAM" = Prosecution Case Management (per the Guidelines' own abbreviations list: "PROCAM: Prosecution Case Management"), and "PROCAMIS" = "Prosecution Case Management Information System." The Guidelines repeatedly instruct that decisions "must be recorded in sufficient detail on PROCAM and/or PROCAMIS," implying PROCAM may denote the manual/paper case-file convention and PROCAMIS its digital counterpart, though the Guidelines do not state this distinction explicitly — the precise relationship between the two names is **UNKNOWN** beyond what's quoted.
- The **police-side reference** is called an **"SD Reference number"** at the point of first police report, per Justice Centres Uganda's public explainer of the criminal process (Justice Centres Uganda is a Justice Law & Order Sector (JLOS) initiative — a government-affiliated but not ODPP-authored source). **[Secondary — Justice Centres Uganda, justicecentres.go.ug]** The same source also uses the term "criminal register number" at the police stage and describes a "criminal register" it labels "PROCAM" at the court-registry stage — this conflicts with ODPP's own definition of PROCAM as its *own* prosecution case-management system, so I treat the ODPP Guidelines as authoritative on what PROCAM/PROCAMIS actually are, and treat the Justice Centres description of the police/court-registry stage terminology (SD Reference number, criminal register number, Police Form 53) as secondary and probably reflects informal or simplified public-facing terminology rather than ODPP's internal system-of-record definitions.

**Bottom line on terminology**: "file" is ODPP's dominant working noun; "case" is used informally alongside it; the police file, the ODPP file, and the court file are three distinct, separately-numbered artifacts describing the same underlying matter.

## 3. The Police → DPP handoff

**Mechanism: "sanctioning."** The handoff is called **sanctioning** in practice. Per Justice Centres Uganda's public explainer of the criminal process in Uganda: once police investigations conclude, "the police file moves to the Director of Public Prosecutions (Resident State Attorney)," which "reviews the charges and evidence to assess whether prosecution is appropriate and whether sufficient evidence exists." The charge sheet used at this stage is identified as **"Police Form 53,"** which "must include the heading, date, police station name, criminal register number, accused's name, offense statement, offense particulars, and officer's signature." **[Secondary — Justice Centres Uganda / JLOS]**

ODPP's own Decision to Charge Guidelines (2023) corroborate and formalise this at a much greater level of detail, and are the authoritative primary source on what "sanctioning" actually requires: **[Primary]**

- **Two-Stage Test.** The default standard: an **Evidential Test** (is there a "realistic prospect of conviction" — assessed on relevance, admissibility, reliability, credibility, availability of evidence, and any defence raised) followed, only if passed, by a **Public Interest Test** (weighing seriousness of the offence, culpability, harm to victim/community, suspect's age, proportionality, availability of out-of-court disposal, and protection of sensitive information).
- **Threshold Test** — a narrower, faster-track standard usable only in "rare and unique circumstances" for very serious cases (sexual/GBV, murder, election violence, anti-corruption, counterterrorism) where an investigation is incomplete but immediate charging is justified and there are grounds to object to bail. Five specific requirements must all be met (reasonable grounds to believe guilt; reasonable grounds further evidence will emerge; seriousness justifying immediate charge; substantial grounds to object to bail; public interest in charging). A decision made this way **must be reviewed within 14 days**, and escalated to a supervisor if the anticipated evidence still hasn't arrived.
- **Minimum requirements of a file under the Threshold Test**: key evidence/information raising reasonable grounds of guilt; a description of outstanding evidence and anticipated timelines; a summary of facts explaining seriousness; and, where relevant, reasons the suspect is a flight/bail risk. If these minimums aren't met, "a charge cannot be preferred," and the Prosecutor must advise investigators what further evidence/steps are needed and the file must be resubmitted.
- **Key evidence expected on the file at point of charge** (non-exhaustive per the Guidelines): all relevant witness statements (including exculpatory ones), statements from investigating/arresting officers, expert evidence, statements of the accused, arrest-related statements, digital evidence (CCTV, audio/visual, metadata), documentary evidence, exhibits. The Prosecutor is explicitly required to check that "the investigation diary is up to date and accurate."
- **What happens procedurally after sanctioning**: "The action officer (Prosecutor) shall sanction the charges by appending his or her signature, date, and stamp on the Charge Sheet. A copy shall be retained in the ODPP PROCAM file. Once the Charge Sheet is filed in court... a court file is opened, and a case number assigned."
- **Withdrawal / return for further investigation**: if minimum requirements aren't met, the Prosecutor "should advise investigators as to what further evidence or steps must be taken," and "the file be resubmitted for further direction" — i.e., a formal return-to-police-for-more-work loop is an established, named ODPP practice, distinct from an outright declination.

**At what stage DPP first sees a matter.** Per the above, ODPP's normal-track involvement is **pre-charge legal review/sanctioning** (Two-Stage Test applied once investigation is essentially complete or, under the Threshold Test, earlier for very serious cases). This directly contradicts a "DPP only sees capital-offence matters at committal" model — **DPP is the gatekeeper who sanctions the charge before it is even filed in the first instance**, for essentially all matters, not merely capital ones. **[Primary]**

**Capital-offence / committal procedure** is a separate, later, additional DPP touchpoint that applies specifically to offences triable in the High Court. Under the Magistrates Courts Act (Chapter 19, current 2023 consolidation), **[Primary — full text fetched and read]**:

- **§168 "Committal for trial by High Court":** "When a person is charged in a magistrate's court with an offence to be tried by the High Court, the Director of Public Prosecutions shall file in the magistrate's court an **indictment** and a **summary of the case** signed by him or her or by an officer authorised by him or her..." The summary of the case "shall contain such particulars as are necessary to give the accused person reasonable information as to the nature of the offence." The magistrate then gives the accused a copy of the indictment and summary, reads them out and explains them, informs the accused they need not plead, and "commit[s] the accused person for trial by the High Court and transmit[s] to the registrar of the High Court copies of the indictment and of the summary of the case." Bail granted by the magistrates court does not lapse on committal (though the High Court may still cancel it).
- **§169 "Director of Public Prosecutions to determine offences to be committed to High Court":** it is "within the discretion of the Director of Public Prosecutions which offences are to be proceeded with... for trial before the High Court or to be tried by a magistrate's court" — i.e., the DPP (not the magistrate) decides venue.
- **§167 "Power to transfer case to superior court":** if the DPP believes, at any stage of magistrate's-court proceedings, that a case ought to be tried by a superior court, the magistrate "shall, on application made by or on behalf of the [DPP]... stop further proceedings and remand the accused person in custody to appear before a superior court."
- **§164 "Committal for sentence":** distinct from committal for *trial* — a magistrate who convicts someone but believes greater punishment is warranted than the court can impose may commit the offender (in custody) to a chief magistrate, or a chief magistrate to the High Court, **for sentencing only**.

**Standard form/register.** A standard charge-sheet form (Police Form 53) is referenced by the JLOS/Justice Centres source **[Secondary]**; ODPP's own Guidelines do not name a specific numbered form but describe the Charge Sheet's required signature/date/stamp workflow and the "ODPP PROCAM file" as the register of record on ODPP's side. Whether there is a single national physical/digital referral register beyond PROCAM/PROCAMIS is **UNKNOWN**.

**Inquest files.** I could not find a primary-source ODPP or statutory description of an inquest-to-DPP referral pathway. The Criminal Procedure Code Act (Chapter 122, as currently consolidated) that I fetched and read in full contains no inquest provisions — its surviving substantive content is now almost entirely about **appeals procedure** (Part III), the arrest/bail/charge provisions having evidently been superseded by/consolidated into the Magistrates Courts Act and Trial on Indictments Act over time. **[Primary — confirmed by reading the full current CPC text]** The Decision to Charge Guidelines mention inquests only once, in passing, as a trigger for reviewing a prior decision not to prosecute: "Cases involving a death in which a review following the findings of an inquest concludes that a prosecution should be brought." **[Primary]** Beyond this, the specific inquest procedure/statute and any standard inquest-file handoff to ODPP is **UNKNOWN**.

## 4. Decision types

Drawing on Article 120 of the Constitution and the Trial on Indictments Act (both fetched and read in full), plus ODPP's own Decision to Charge Guidelines: **[Primary throughout this section unless marked]**

| Decision | Legal basis | Who has authority | Delegable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sanction/charge** (decide to prosecute) | Art. 120(3)(b); ODPP Guidelines Ch. 2-3 | DPP or authorised Prosecutor ("action officer") | Yes — Art. 120(4) covers (a)-(c); routinely exercised by State Attorneys/RSAs in practice |
| **Decline to charge / "not to charge"** | ODPP Guidelines §2.9, §2.11.6 | Prosecutor, with written reasons; may be overturned later (see below) | Yes, in practice — subject to supervisory review |
| **Alternatives to prosecution / diversion** (caution, apology/reconciliation, compensation, referral to diversion programme) | ODPP Guidelines Ch. 5 | "Action officer/Prosecutor" makes the initial decision, reasons recorded in writing; may be reviewed by a supervisor | Yes |
| **Threshold Test charge** (charge before investigation complete, very serious cases only) | ODPP Guidelines §2.4 | Prosecutor, "only after consultation with the immediate supervisor" | Requires supervisor consultation, not DPP-exclusive |
| **Return for further investigation** | ODPP Guidelines §2.5.3, §2.11.7 | Prosecutor gives written advice to Investigating Officer; file resubmitted | Yes |
| **Committal for trial (High Court)** | Constitution Art. 120 (High Court jurisdiction requires committal); Magistrates Courts Act §168-169 | DPP (or authorised officer) files indictment + summary of case; DPP decides venue (§169) | Filing/venue decision delegable per Art.120(4)(b); see MCA §168(1) "or by an officer authorised by him or her" |
| **Take over prosecution instituted by another person/authority** | Constitution Art. 120(3)(c) | DPP or authorised officer | Delegable (Art. 120(4) covers (a)-(c)) |
| **Discontinue proceedings (before judgment)** | Constitution Art. 120(3)(d) | **DPP personally, exclusively** — Art. 120(4) does not list (d) among delegable functions; ODPP's own Guidelines confirm: "the mandate to withdraw charges is exercised exclusively by the [DPP]" | **Not delegable** |
| **Nolle prosequi** (a specific form of discontinuance, at High Court stage) | Trial on Indictments Act §134 | **DPP personally only** — TIA §135 explicitly states the Deputy DPP, a state attorney, or a state prosecutor may exercise any DPP power under the Act **"except the power to enter a nolle prosequi."** | **Not delegable — DPP only** |
| **Plea bargain / accept a guilty plea** | ODPP Guidelines Ch. 4; Judicature (Plea Bargain) Rules, 2016 (S.I. No. 43 of 2016) | Prosecutor negotiates; "the decision rests with the Prosecutor," subject to court approval of any recommended sentence | Delegable in practice (a dedicated "Plea Bargain Unit" exists within General Case Work — see §7) |
| **Direct alternative/amended charges** | ODPP Guidelines §3.1 (charge selection); MCA §88 / TIA §25 (framing rules) | Prosecutor, "proactive in seeking an amendment if it is in the interests of justice" | Delegable |

**Nolle prosequi — procedure and effect (Trial on Indictments Act §134, quoted from the fetched primary text):**

> "(1) In any case committed for trial to the High Court, and at any stage thereof before verdict, the Director of Public Prosecutions may enter a nolle prosequi, either by stating in court or by informing the court in writing that the State intends that the proceedings shall not continue, and thereupon the accused shall be at once discharged in respect of the charge for which the nolle prosequi is entered, and if he or she has been committed to prison shall be released, or if on bail his or her recognisances shall be discharged; **but such discharge of an accused person shall not operate as a bar to any subsequent proceedings against him or her on account of the same facts.**
> (2) If the accused is not before the court when such nolle prosequi is entered, the chief registrar or clerk of the court shall immediately cause notice in writing of the entry of the nolle prosequi to be given to the keeper of the prison in which the accused may be detained, and also, if the accused person has been committed for trial, to the magistrates' court by which he or she was so committed, and the magistrates' court shall immediately cause a similar notice in writing to be given to any witnesses bound over to prosecute and give evidence and to their sureties, if any, and also to the accused and his or her sureties in case he or she shall have been admitted to bail."

Key effect: a nolle prosequi discharges the accused from custody/bail on that charge but is **not an acquittal** and does not bar future re-prosecution on the same facts. It is procedurally distinct from — but a species of — the general Article 120(3)(d) discontinuance power, and applies specifically to cases already committed to the High Court.

**Reviews and re-opening a declined charge.** ODPP's Guidelines (§2.9) list explicit circumstances that can trigger overturning a prior decision not to prosecute: further review shows the original decision was wrong; new/more significant evidence emerges later; or "a review following the findings of an inquest concludes that a prosecution should be brought." All such reviews "must be recorded in writing," and written reasons for declining are subject to strict confidentiality — "Written reviews MUST NEVER be given to unauthorised persons," being protected by legal professional privilege between the investigating agency and ODPP.

**Continuous review triggers** (i.e., events that should prompt an ODPP prosecutor to re-examine an ongoing case): three consecutive prosecution-caused adjournments; new evidence/information surfacing; witnesses failing to attend or becoming unavailable; disclosure obligations arising. **[Primary — ODPP Guidelines §2.8]**

## 5. DPP ↔ Courts relationship

**What ODPP needs to track about the court side**, per the Decision to Charge Guidelines and Magistrates Courts Act/Trial on Indictments Act text: **[Primary]**

- The **court case number**, assigned when the Charge Sheet is filed and recorded on both the ODPP's copy of the Charge Sheet and the ODPP file (Guidelines §3.3.2).
- **Committal status** — whether/when a matter has been committed for trial to the High Court, since High Court jurisdiction is conditioned on committal (TIA §1; MCA §168).
- **Trial date notice** — TIA §27(2): "The registrar of the High Court shall give the accused person and the Director of Public Prosecutions reasonable notice, not being less than fourteen days, of the date of the trial."
- **Adjournments** — tracked as a trigger for mandatory internal review: "three consecutive adjournments, by the prosecution, should trigger a review" (Guidelines §2.8.2).
- **Bail status** — the Guidelines note ODPP must track objections to bail (esp. under the Threshold Test) and that bail granted by a magistrates court "shall not lapse on the committal" to High Court (MCA §168(4)), though the High Court may cancel it (§168(5)).
- **Plea and case-progress status** generally (plea entered, trial in progress, judgment/sentence), implied throughout the "continuing review of ongoing prosecutions" provisions, though no single canonical list of court-outcome states is given in ODPP's own documents I could access.

**Existing Judiciary case-management system.** Uganda's judiciary operates the **Electronic Court Case Management Information System (ECCMIS)** (also referred to elsewhere as "e-Judiciary"), launched with a "Go-Live" by the Chief Justice on 1 March 2022, per Judiciary of Uganda's own implementation-review report and news pages. **[Primary — I fetched judiciary.go.ug's own ECCMIS description page directly.]** Per that same ODPP-adjacent secondary source (an academic/IACA journal article on Uganda court digitisation, and the vendor Synisys's own project announcements): **[Secondary]** ECCMIS is intended to integrate across justice-sector institutions, including ODPP, police, prisons, NIRA, and Ministry of Lands, with a stated ambition that "integration between PROCAMIS (ODPP system), ePIS (police), and the ECCMIS in the judiciary would add significant value to the criminal case management process." **Caution**: this is framed as an aspiration/value-case in the sources I found, not confirmed as a live, operational, bidirectional data integration — the judiciary's own ECCMIS overview page I fetched directly describes ECCMIS's internal features (e-filing, SMS/USSD access, automated case processing, reporting) but **does not itself mention PROCAMIS integration at all**. Whether PROCAMIS and ECCMIS are actually integrated today, and to what extent, is **UNKNOWN** — a Pleadli DPP design should not assume a live system-to-system feed exists and should treat court-side data as something ODPP staff track independently (manually or via PROCAMIS) rather than something guaranteed to sync automatically from the judiciary side.

## 6. Documents

Confirmed ODPP/prosecution documents, with primary sourcing given: **[Primary unless marked]**

- **Charge Sheet** — filed in a magistrate's court; sanctioned by the Prosecutor's "signature, date, and stamp" (ODPP Guidelines §3.3.1); framing rules governed by Magistrates Courts Act §86-88 (joinder of counts, joinder of persons, rules for framing charges — all read in full from the fetched MCA text). Publicly referred to as "Police Form 53" at the point of police drafting. **[Secondary — Justice Centres Uganda for the form number]**
- **Indictment** — used for High Court trials; governed by Trial on Indictments Act Part III (§§22-27, all read in full): must state the specific offence(s) with sufficient particulars (§22); rules on joinder of counts (§23) and joinder of persons (§24); detailed framing rules (§25, (a)-(q) — e.g. plain-language "statement of offence," no need to negative exceptions, permitted alternative-fact pleading, etc.); **must be in the name of, and (subject to §135 delegation of signature) signed by, the DPP** (§26); has a prescribed opening form addressed to the High Court (§27).
- **Summary of the case** — a distinct document filed alongside the indictment at committal, per MCA §168(1)-(2): "contain[s] such particulars as are necessary to give the accused person reasonable information as to the nature of the offence."
- **Legal opinion / written review** — ODPP Guidelines §1.6 (Record Keeping) and §2.10 (Confidentiality of Written Reviews): prosecutors must keep written records of decisions and instructions, filed on PROCAM/PROCAMIS; written reviews are privileged between the investigating agency and ODPP and must never be given to unauthorised persons; reasons for declining to prosecute may be communicated to complainants/families only via a supervisor-approved written letter referencing the Guidelines.
- **Plea agreement** — governed by the **Judicature (Plea Bargain) Rules, 2016** (Statutory Instrument No. 43 of 2016, published in the Uganda Gazette, Vol. CIX, No. 45, dated 24 June 2016). I confirmed this instrument's existence and citation via ULII and the Judiciary of Uganda's own downloads page, and downloaded the Judiciary's own copy of the Rules PDF, but **could not extract its section-by-section text** (the PDF appears to be an image scan without an extractable text layer, and available tooling in this environment had no OCR capability). What I *can* confirm from ODPP's own Guidelines (§4, read in full): a plea agreement may be entered between the Prosecutor and an accused who has been charged in court, at any time before judgment; prosecutors "must be familiar with and apply the Plea-Bargaining Rules"; prosecutors must never accept a plea merely for convenience, must ensure the court retains adequate sentencing/confiscation/ancillary-order powers, must take particular care around pleas that would let an accused avoid a mandatory minimum sentence, should consider victim/family views (though the final decision rests with the Prosecutor), and must make clear to the court the factual basis on which any plea is advanced. **Current status**: multiple independent secondary sources (CEPIL – Center for Public Interest Law; an academic paper on domestic/IPV plea bargaining; ODPP's own dedicated "Plea Bargain Unit" — see §7) treat plea bargaining as a real, currently-operating practice in Uganda, not a discontinued pilot — the pilot-to-institutionalisation transition itself is **[Secondary]**, but the *existence of a live, dedicated ODPP Plea Bargain Unit today* is **[Primary]**, sourced from ODPP's own current directorate page.
- **Committal proceedings documents** — the indictment and summary of case, transmitted by the magistrate to the High Court registrar per MCA §168(3)(c) (quoted above).
- **Submission of no case to answer** — referenced generically in the Trial on Indictments Act's trial-procedure Part (VII, "Close of case for the prosecution" §73, and defence procedure thereafter, §74) but the Act does not use the exact phrase "no case to answer" in the sections I read; the concept is implicit in the structure (prosecution closes its case, defence may respond) rather than named as a distinct document type in the primary text I obtained. **UNKNOWN** whether ODPP has a standard internal template/form for responding to or anticipating a no-case-to-answer submission.

## 8. Service standards / metrics

**ODPP's own internally-documented operational timing rules** (the closest thing to a citable "service standard" I could find in an ODPP-authored document): **[Primary — ODPP Decision to Charge Guidelines]**

- A Threshold Test charging decision **"must be kept under regular review and the first review must take place within 14 days"** of the decision; if anticipated evidence still hasn't materialised, the case must be escalated to a supervisor for direction.
- **Three consecutive prosecution-caused adjournments** should automatically trigger a case review.
- Ongoing prosecutions must be kept under continuous review whenever new evidence/information surfaces, a witness becomes unavailable, or disclosure obligations require it.
- All decisions and reviews must be recorded in writing and filed on PROCAM/PROCAMIS "in sufficient detail" — implying an expectation of auditable, timestamped case records as a baseline compliance standard, though no specific SLA/turnaround metric (e.g., "X days from referral to sanctioning decision") is stated anywhere in the ODPP document I obtained.

**I could not locate an ODPP-published annual/strategic report** with disposal-rate, backlog, or caseload statistics, despite searching specifically for ODPP annual reports 2023/2024. **UNKNOWN** whether ODPP publishes one publicly, or it simply wasn't surfaced by the searches available to me.

**What I did find, and its limits**: the **Uganda Police Force's own 2024 Annual Crime Report** (a Police document, not an ODPP one) contains statistics that describe ODPP's downstream effect on case flow, e.g.: "32.2 percent (26,328) of all cases taken to Court secured convictions"; "53.3 percent (43,579) of all cases taken to Court are still pending"; and — most relevant to sanctioning throughput — "only 37.4 percent [of cases reported to Police in 2024] were fully investigated by Police, presented to [ODPP], were sanctioned by the ODPP and were taken to Court," while "41.5 percent... remained still under inquiry." **[Secondary — Uganda Police Force Annual Crime Report 2024, upf.go.ug]** These are Police-authored figures describing the whole pipeline, not ODPP's own published KPIs, and should not be presented as ODPP's official service standards — but they are a reasonable secondary proxy for what "backlog" and "throughput" look like at the police→ODPP→court boundary, and directly suggest that "cases pending investigation," "cases pending sanctioning," and "cases pending court disposal" are the three natural bucket-states worth modelling for a work-queue view.

# Uganda Legal Profession & Defense Practice — Research Brief

Research conducted to ground the design of "Pleadli Lawyers," the private/defense-practice member of the Pleadli family of purpose-built Uganda justice-system apps, sibling to Pleadli Police and Pleadli DPP. This is the first foundational research pass for the Lawyers side. Structured by the six question areas set out in the research brief, plus a References section.

**Confidence tags used throughout, matching `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` convention:** **[Primary]** = verified against the actual statutory/official-document text fetched and read in full (or in relevant part) during this research. **[Secondary]** = drawn from a named secondary source (news, NGO, academic paper, law-firm commentary, or a search-engine summary of a source this research could not itself open) because a primary source could not be located, could not be read, or did not establish the detail independently. **UNKNOWN** = not established by any source found; stated plainly rather than guessed.

**A note on tooling limits, consistent with the DPP research's own finding:** several official PDFs in this space (notably the Judicature (Plea Bargain) Rules 2016 itself, hosted at judiciary.go.ug) are image scans with no extractable text layer, and no OCR tool was available in this research environment (no `tesseract`, no `pdftoppm`/`convert`, and package installation is not permitted). Where the Rules' content is described below, it is reconstructed from **multiple independent secondary sources** (academic papers, CEPIL's own explainer, search-engine-indexed excerpts of academic course documents) that consistently cite the same rule numbers (Rules 3, 4, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14; Schedules 1 and 3) — this is stronger corroboration than a single secondary source, but it is not the same as this research having read the primary text itself, and is labeled **[Secondary]** throughout.

## 1. Legal mandate/structure of the profession

**Two different bodies, with different roles — this is a real, primary-source-confirmed distinction, not a naming quirk.**

**The Law Council** is the statutory *regulator*. It is established by the **Advocates Act, Cap 267** (fetched and read in full from the Ministry of Justice/business-licensing official host, businesslicences.go.ug), §2(1): **[Primary]**

> "There is established a Law Council which shall consist of—
> (a) a judge, appointed by the Attorney General after consultation with the Chief Justice, who shall be chairperson of the council;
> (b) the president of the Uganda Law Society, ex officio;
> (c) the director of the Law Development Centre, ex officio;
> (d) the head of the department of law of Makerere University, ex officio;
> (e) two practising advocates elected by the Uganda Law Society; and
> (f) one officer with legal qualifications in the service of the Government, appointed by the Attorney General."

Its functions (Advocates Act §3) are, verbatim: **[Primary]**

> "(a) to exercise general supervision and control over professional legal education in Uganda;
> (b) ... to approve courses of study and to provide for the conduct of qualifying examinations ...;
> (c) to advise and make recommendations to the Government on matters relating to the profession of advocates;
> (d) to exercise, through the medium of the Disciplinary Committee, disciplinary control over advocates and their clerks;
> (e) to exercise general supervision and control over the provision of legal aid and advice to indigent persons; and
> (f) to exercise any power or perform any duty authorised or required by this or any other written law."

**The Uganda Law Society (ULS)** is a *separate body corporate* — the professional association/bar society, not the regulator — established by its own statute, the **Uganda Law Society Act, Cap 276** (commencement 27 December 1956; fetched and read in full from ULS's own official site, uls.or.ug). **[Primary]** Its objects (§3) are to maintain/improve professional standards and learning, represent/protect/assist members "as regards conditions of practice," protect and assist the public, and assist Government/courts on legislation and practice of law. Its governance is a Council of president, vice-president, the Attorney General and Solicitor General (ex officio, either eligible to be elected president), secretary, treasurer and four other elected members (§9). Membership (§4) is open to anyone entitled to practice under Advocates Act §6(2), and is compulsory (not merely elective) for certain public-service legal officers designated by the Minister. Critically, §14 of the ULS Act states: "All representatives of the Society on the Law Council shall be elected by the society in general meeting" — confirming the interlock: ULS elects some Law Council members and its president sits on the Law Council ex officio, but ULS itself has **no direct disciplinary or licensing power** under either Act. **[Primary]**

**The relationship is bidirectional but asymmetric**: the Law Council regulates the profession and administers discipline; the ULS is the members' professional body which also happens to hold guaranteed representation on the regulator's board. This mirrors common-law "Bar Council vs. Bar Association" splits elsewhere, though Uganda's specific arrangement (ULS president sitting ex officio on the Law Council, rather than the Law Council being wholly independent of the association) is closer than most. **[Primary — my own synthesis of the two Acts' text, not itself claimed by either source]**

**Admission/enrollment** (Advocates Act, Part IV, §§7–12, read in full): **[Primary]**
- The registrar keeps a "roll of advocates" (§7).
- To be entered on the roll, an applicant must have "complied with such requirements (whether relating to instruction, examination or otherwise) as to the acquisition of professional skill and experience as may be specified in regulations made ... by the Law Council" (§8(1)) — i.e., the Advocates Act itself does not specify the Bar Course/Law Development Centre requirement in its own text; that is delegated to Law Council regulations. The requirement that Ugandan advocates typically complete a Bar Course at the Law Development Centre (LDC) is **well-established as common professional knowledge and corroborated by the LDC's ex officio Law Council seat (§2(1)(c) above) and by LASPNET's own fact sheet, which separately describes LDC's role in legal-aid clinical training** — but the Bar Course requirement's exact statutory/regulatory citation was not independently read in full during this research. **[Secondary for the specific "Bar Course at LDC" mechanism; Primary for the delegation-to-regulations structure itself]**
- Eligible applicants apply to the Law Council, which if satisfied issues a certificate; the applicant then applies to the **Chief Justice** to be entered on the roll, on payment of a fee (§8(2)-(3)) — so enrollment is nominally a Chief Justice act, gated by a Law Council certificate.
- Every enrolled advocate must separately obtain an annual **practising certificate** from the registrar (§11), valid to 31 December each year and renewable. The registrar **must refuse** to issue/renew a practising certificate if the advocate is an undischarged bankrupt, adjudged of unsound mind, has unpaid fines/costs under the Act, has not complied with Law Council accounting regulations, or — notably — **"has not paid his or her subscription as a member of the Uganda Law Society"** (§12(1)(e)). This is a concrete, primary-source-confirmed mechanism tying ULS membership dues to the legal ability to practice, even though ULS itself is not the licensing body.

**Disciplinary mechanism** (Advocates Act, §§18-20, read in full): **[Primary]**
- The **Disciplinary Committee** is established "for the purposes of this Act" and consists of: the Law Council member appointed under §2(1)(f) (the government legal officer), ex officio; plus four other Law Council members — one drawn from the two ULS-elected advocates (§2(1)(e)), and three from the Law Council's other members (§18(1)). Quorum is three; the Law Council's secretary also serves as the Committee's secretary (§18(7)).
- A **complaint against an advocate for professional misconduct may be made to the Disciplinary Committee by the Law Council or by any person** (§20(1)) — i.e., a client, opposing party, or member of the public can lodge a complaint directly, not only the regulator itself.
- The Committee has power to administer oaths, issue summonses for evidence/documents, and its proceedings are deemed "judicial proceedings" for Penal Code purposes (§19); a party may be represented by an advocate, or the Committee may appoint one (§19(2)).
- Orders of the Committee are admissible in evidence (§36) and non-compliance is penalized (§38); members have statutory immunity for acts done in good faith (§39).

**Professional conduct rules**: a distinct instrument, the **Advocates (Professional Conduct) Regulations** (Statutory Instrument 267-2, made under the Advocates Act), governs day-to-day professional-conduct duties — described consistently by multiple independent secondary/academic sources as covering the fiduciary advocate-client relationship, duty of diligent advice, confidentiality, competence, and (per Studocu-hosted course material) some 31 distinct conduct rules. **[Secondary — I located and confirmed the instrument's existence/citation (SI 267-2) via multiple sources, including a repository listing at repository.globethics.net, but could not myself fetch and read its full operative text in this research; treat the specific "31 codes" figure and the detailed content summary as secondary, not independently verified against the Regulations' own text.]** This instrument is directly relevant to Pleadli Lawyers because it is referenced by name, as binding law, inside the 2022 State Funded Legal Representation Rules discussed in §4 below (e.g. governing when a state-appointed advocate may decline or must withdraw from a case).

A related, separately-named instrument — the **Advocates (Pro Bono Services to Indigent Persons) Regulations, 2009** (Law Council SI No. 39 of 2009) — requires every advocate in good standing to provide **40 hours per calendar year** of free legal services to indigent persons, administered/coordinated through a Uganda Law Society project (the "ULS Pro Bono Scheme"). **[Secondary — consistently described across ULS's own "Pro Bono Scheme" page and independent academic/NGO sources, but the Regulations' own text was not independently fetched and read in this research.]** This is a second, independent primary-adjacent example of the ULS/Law Council interlock: a Law Council regulation whose administration is delegated in practice to the bar association.

## 2. How a private practice/law firm's work is actually organized

**Terminology.** No Uganda-specific statute or ULS/Law Council publication found in this research defines a formal term for a lawyer's individual unit of work (equivalent to ODPP's defined "file" in `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md`). In the sources read, Ugandan practitioners and academic writing use "file," "matter," and "case" essentially interchangeably and informally — none is used as a formally defined term of art the way ODPP's Decision to Charge Guidelines formally define "file." **UNKNOWN / not formally defined** — this should not be assumed to have the same rigor as ODPP's own internal terminology.

**Client-matter numbering convention.** Searched specifically for a Uganda-wide or ULS-published standard for matter/file numbering at law firms. **No such standard was found.** General law-firm practice-management literature (Clio, PracticePanther, and similar international legal-tech vendors) describes matter-numbering as a firm-by-firm internal practice-management choice everywhere, and nothing found suggests Uganda has any Law Council- or ULS-mandated numbering scheme analogous to, say, ODPP's "ODPP registration number" or the police "PF2A/2B Case File Number." **UNKNOWN — treat as firm-by-firm, with no evidence of any Uganda-wide standard; do not invent one for the product.**

**Shape of a Ugandan lawyer's caseload.** The clearest, most concrete evidence found is negative: a list of prominent Ugandan-headquartered law firms (Wikipedia's "List of law firms in Uganda," cross-checked against individual firm pages) shows Uganda's largest firms are **full-service commercial practices** — e.g. Kampala Associated Advocates (KAA), described in its own page as "one of the largest law firms in Uganda" with "26 lawyers, of whom 15 are partners" (as of 2020) across practice areas including **Commercial Litigation and Arbitration, Telecommunications/Media/Technology, Energy and Natural Resources, Tax, Corporate Advisory, Employment, IP, Banking and Finance, Capital Markets, M&A, and Mining**. **[Secondary — Wikipedia, itself citing firm/press sources; not independently verified against KAA's own site in this research]** Nothing in KAA's listed practice areas is criminal defense — this is consistent with (though does not conclusively prove) a picture where Uganda's largest, most commercially prestigious firms are civil/commercial-transactional in character, and criminal defense work sits elsewhere in the profession (smaller firms, sole practitioners, or specific litigation-oriented chambers) rather than being a headline practice area of the "top-tier" firms.

The Uganda Law Society's own 2023/2024 Annual Report (read in full, 194 pages, extracted via ghostscript from the PDF) organizes its member-facing practice-development activity into **"Clusters"** — described as "practice specialty groups" commissioned by the ULS Council "whose objective is to enrich members' careers ... and promote member involvement." Named clusters/events found in the report include a **Family Law Cluster**, an **Intellectual Property Cluster**, and a **Tax Cluster**, plus a one-off "Criminal Law Webinar" CLE event (not itself named as a standing "cluster"). **[Primary — from ULS's own Annual Report]** **No dedicated "Criminal Law Cluster" or "criminal defence lawyers' association" was found named anywhere in the ULS Annual Report or in general search for a Uganda-specific criminal-defense bar subgroup.** This is a real finding of absence, not proof that none exists (the report's cluster list, as excerpted, may not be exhaustive) — flagged as **UNKNOWN whether a dedicated criminal-defense specialty grouping exists within or alongside ULS**, with the concrete evidence pointing toward criminal defense being handled as one strand of general litigation practice rather than as a formally separated bar subgroup akin to, e.g., a "Criminal Bar Association" in some other common-law jurisdictions.

**Bottom line for product design**: the evidence assembled (largest firms' practice-area lists being commercial/corporate-heavy; no dedicated criminal-defense ULS cluster found; the state-brief/legal-aid system in §4 below relying on individually-recruited advocates rather than dedicated criminal-defense firms) is consistent with criminal defense in Uganda being practiced as **one matter type mixed into general litigation practice** — by sole practitioners, small general-practice firms, and individual advocates who also take state-brief/pro-bono criminal instructions — rather than as a distinct specialized track. This inference should be treated as **corroborated-but-not-conclusive**; it was not stated as such by any single source.

## 3. Plea bargaining from the defense side

**Statutory basis and objectives.** The **Judicature (Plea Bargain) Rules, 2016** (Statutory Instrument No. 43 of 2016, Uganda Gazette Vol. CIX No. 45, 24 June 2016) is confirmed to exist and is stably hosted by the Judiciary of Uganda (judiciary.go.ug) and cited by ULII — **[Primary]** for existence/citation — but, as `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` also found, **the Judiciary's own hosted PDF is an image scan with no extractable text layer**, and no OCR tool was available in this research environment to independently read it. Everything below about the Rules' operative content is therefore **[Secondary]**, reconstructed from multiple independent academic/NGO sources that consistently cite the same rule numbers — a meaningfully stronger form of secondary corroboration than a single source, but still not this research having read the primary text.

**Objectives (Rule 3)**, per an academic paper (IJFMR, 2026) that quotes the Rules directly: **[Secondary]**
> "To enhance the efficiency of the criminal justice system ...; To enable the accused and the prosecution in consultation with the victim, to reach an amicable agreement on an appropriate punishment; To facilitate reduction in case backlog and prison congestion; To provide quick relief from the anxiety of criminal prosecution; To encourage accused persons to own up to their criminal responsibility; and To involve the victim in the adjudication process."

**Definition (Rule 4)**: "the process between an accused person and the prosecution, in which the accused person agrees to plead guilty in exchange for an agreement by the prosecutor to drop one or more charges, reduce a charge to a less serious offence, or recommend a particular sentence acceptable to the accused subject to approval by Court." **[Secondary, same source]** — this matches ODPP's own Guidelines Ch. 4 as already established in `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md`.

**Does the process require legal representation, or specify a defense-counsel role?** Yes, and this is the central finding for the product question. Multiple independent secondary sources converge on the same specific mechanism: **[Secondary — convergent across CEPIL (a Ugandan public-interest-law NGO), an ijfmr.com academic paper, and search-engine-indexed excerpts of Uganda law-school course material citing specific rule/schedule numbers]**

- Before a plea bargain agreement is signed by the accused, it **"shall ... be explained to the accused person by his or her advocate or a justice of the peace in a language that the accused understands."** This is a real, specific, and load-bearing detail: the explanatory/advisory role is not exclusively an advocate's — a justice of the peace is named as an alternative, implying the Rules anticipate that some accused persons proceeding to plea bargain will **not** have an advocate, and provide a non-lawyer substitute for the explanation duty rather than making legal representation an absolute precondition.
- The **plea bargain agreement itself (Rule 9, Schedule 1 form)** is described consistently as containing: the accused's rights and a waiver of those rights, biographical information, a case summary, agreed facts, mitigating and aggravating factors, the accused's plea, **and statements from both the accused's advocate and the prosecuting agent supporting the plea agreement** — i.e., it is structurally a document with a place for defense counsel's own statement, not merely the prosecutor's. **The form is signed by the prosecutor, the accused, and the accused's advocate** (where the accused has one).
- A **separate, distinct document — the Plea Bargain Confirmation (Rule 12(5), Schedule 3 form)** — is signed by "the parties" before the presiding judicial officer, at which point it "become[s] part of the court record" and is "binding on the prosecution and the accused."
- **This directly answers the "jointly authored/signed by both sides, or produced by one side" sub-question: the plea agreement is structurally a jointly-signed, jointly-authored document with dedicated fields for the defense advocate's own supporting statement — not a DPP-drafted instrument that the defense merely countersigns.** This is the single most important product-design finding in this research: the plea agreement is not "ODPP's document" in the way the Charge Sheet or Indictment are (per `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §6) — it is co-authored.
- Court commentary quoted in the same academic paper (from *Lwanga Suleman alias Katongole v. Uganda*, 2019, a Ugandan High Court case) states: "...ideally plea bargain should be at the time of plea taking to enable **the state, the accused and defence counsel** agree on amending the charge sheet or indictment where necessary..." — again naming defence counsel as a co-equal party to the negotiation, not a bystander to a prosecutor-and-accused-only process.

**Withdrawal and court oversight**: Rule 13 gives the court discretion to reject a plea agreement; Rule 14 allows either party to withdraw before sentence is pronounced. **[Secondary]**

**Is legal representation actually required, or can an unrepresented accused plea-bargain?** The "advocate **or** a justice of the peace" explanation clause (above) is the strongest specific textual signal found, and it points toward representation being the norm/expectation but **not an absolute jailbreak-clause requirement** — an unrepresented accused's plea bargain can apparently still proceed with a justice of the peace performing the explanation role instead of an advocate. **No source found stated an outright "an accused may not plea bargain without an advocate" rule**, and this should be treated as **UNKNOWN/not confirmed either way** rather than assumed. This finding is significant for product scope: it implies Pleadli Lawyers, if it models plea bargaining, is modeling the *represented* path through a *process that does not statutorily require* representation — the unrepresented path (with a JP performing the explanation function) is a real, named alternative the product should not assume away.

**Legal representation as a constitutional backdrop.** Article 28(3) of the Constitution of Uganda, 1995 (fetched via a constitution-mirror site, cross-checked against a widely-quoted paraphrase) enumerates the rights of an accused person, including (3)(e): entitlement to **"legal representation at the expense of the State"** specifically for offences carrying death or life imprisonment — for all other offences, the accused may appear "in person or, at that person's own expense, by a lawyer of his or her choice." **[Primary — direct constitutional text, consistent with the same Article already used in `docs/police/PRODUCT.md`-adjacent research]** This is the constitutional hook for the state-funded-representation scheme detailed in §4 below, and confirms that outside capital/life-imprisonment cases, an accused person (plea-bargaining or not) has **no constitutional entitlement to a lawyer at all** unless they can pay privately or access a legal-aid/pro-bono channel.

## 4. Legal aid / access to counsel

This is the area where this research found the richest, most directly primary material — and it materially changes the shape of who a "Lawyers" app user might be.

**A real, current, primary-source-verified state scheme exists and was substantially reformed in 2022.** The **Judicature (Legal Representation at the Expense of the State) Rules, 2022** (Statutory Instrument 2022 No. 55, made under Judicature Act §41, Uganda Gazette Vol. CXV No. 37, 28 June 2022) is a genuinely primary source: it was **hosted as a proper text-layer PDF (not an image scan) and was fetched and read in full** during this research — a meaningfully stronger source than the Plea Bargain Rules discussed above. **[Primary]**

Key structure of the scheme, quoted/paraphrased directly from the Rules' own text:

- **Scope (Rule 2)**: applies to "representation for accused persons at the expense of the state for criminal offences which carry a sentence of death or imprisonment for life" — i.e., this scheme operationalizes Article 28(3)(e) specifically, and does **not** cover ordinary (non-capital) criminal defendants.
- **Governance**: a **State Funded Legal Representation Implementation Committee** (Rule 5) chaired by the Chief Registrar, and including: the Secretary to the Judiciary; a DPP representative; the Registrars of the Supreme Court, Court of Appeal, High Court, and Magistrates Affairs; **the President of the Uganda Law Society**; the Secretary of the Law Council; a civil-society legal-aid representative; and a practicing advocate nominated by the ULS President. **This is a fourth concrete example of the ULS/Law Council/Judiciary/DPP interlock** — the scheme is explicitly cross-institutional by its own governance design, not owned by any single Pleadli-modeled institution.
- **A "Roll of State Appointed Advocates"** (Rule 7) is maintained by the Chief Registrar; recruitment happens annually **"through the Uganda Law Society"** issuing expressions-of-interest notices. Eligibility (Rule 8) requires a minimum of 1 year practice experience for Magistrates Court matters, 2 years for High Court, 5 years for Court of Appeal/Supreme Court matters, and no record of prior professional misconduct.
- **Appointment mechanics** (Rules 9, 12): a Registrar/Chief Magistrate appoints from the Roll on a **rotational basis** (Rule 12(8)) considering geography, experience, case complexity, and cost efficiency; an advocate not on the Roll may still be appointed only if acting pro bono under the 2009 Pro Bono Regulations (§1 above) or where the court deems it in the interest of justice with the accused's consent.
- **Duties of the state-appointed advocate** (Rule 13) are specific and enforceable: meet with the accused to advise/counsel; confirm that meeting to court in writing within 2 working days; submit a **"certificate of trial readiness"** (Schedule 2 form) within 5 working days before hearing — and this certificate is explicitly **"completed by the prosecutor and the defence advocate" jointly** (Rule 13(5)) — a second concrete example (alongside the plea agreement form in §3) of a document structurally requiring both sides' input. An appointed advocate remains attached to the case through pre-trial, trial, and (where practicable) appeal/revision/review (Rule 16), unless the convict objects.
- **Removal from the Roll** (Rule 10) is tied directly back into the Advocates Act disciplinary machinery: an advocate is removed if "found guilty of professional misconduct by the Law Council," disbarred, found incompetent, or reported for unethical conduct.
- **Remuneration** (Rule 17): paid per Schedule 3 fee rates, separately for trial and for any appeal; disputes go to the Chief Registrar and then the Committee. Advocates acting under the separate 2009 Pro Bono Regulations are **not** paid under these Rules (i.e., pro bono and state-funded-representation are two distinct, non-overlapping payment tracks even though both route through the same Roll/eligibility machinery in places).
- **Ethical withdrawal** (Rule 15) is tied to the Advocates (Professional Conduct) Regulations by name: where those Regulations or statute require an advocate to withdraw, the advocate must inform the court, which then appoints a replacement from the Roll.

**How this compares to the older, informally-described "state brief" system.** A LASPNET fact sheet ("Legal Aid Service Providers Network Fact Sheet on Legal Aid in Uganda," fetched and read in full via ghostscript extraction; undated but internally referencing a 2016 HiiL report and a 2017 UPF crime report, so likely produced circa 2017-2018) describes the pre-2022 "state brief scheme" in blunter, more critical terms: **[Secondary — LASPNET, an NGO umbrella body, not a government publication]**
> "The State brief scheme[,] as provided for under article 28 of the constitution[.] In this case lawyers are retained by government/judiciary to defend suspects with capital offences. This scheme is challenged by poor remuneration of advocates, inadequate preparation of some of the lawyers and lack of client-lawyer confidence. A lawyer is given between 150,000 to 250,000 [Ugandan shillings, roughly USD 40-70 at the time] to provide legal services. This is in essence a pro-bono service and quality [of] services [is] not assured."

Read together, the 2022 Rules read as a **direct institutional response to exactly these named criticisms** (formal Roll, minimum-experience eligibility, rotational assignment to prevent favoritism, a documented remuneration schedule, monitoring/feedback forms from the represented accused themselves) — though this specific causal claim (that the 2022 Rules were drafted in response to LASPNET's critique) is **my own inference**, not stated by either source, and should be treated as **UNKNOWN as a causal claim** even though both documents are independently well-sourced.

**Other legal-aid channels for non-capital, indigent accused persons** (who fall outside both the Article 28(3)(e)/2022-Rules scheme and any private-lawyer arrangement), per the same LASPNET fact sheet: **[Secondary]**
- **Justice Centres Uganda** — a Ministry of Justice/JLOS-affiliated pilot program (already independently corroborated as a source in `docs/police/PRODUCT.md`-adjacent research) employing salaried staff advocates, paralegals, and psychosocial-support officers, described by LASPNET as "90% donor funded and hence not sustainable" under current funding arrangements.
- **The Law Development Centre's Legal Aid Clinic** — a "student advocate model" using LDC Bar Course students, "majorly donor funded."
- **Makerere University's Public Interest Law Clinic (PILAC)** — a similar student-clinic model.
- **The Uganda Law Society's own Pro-Bono Scheme** — per the fact sheet, "over 1633 lawyers ... voluntarily enrolled for Pro bono services," including criminal-justice-area work, "based on the mercy and willingness of an advocate" with no guaranteed quality assurance.
- **The Paralegal Advisory Services** (managed by the Foundation for Human Rights Initiative, FHRI) — paralegals (not advocates) who help suspects navigate the system and connect with relatives/lawyers; this is a paralegal, not lawyer, channel.
- **LASPNET itself** is described as a network umbrella of "over 40" (per an earlier-cited Devex/Wikipedia description) or, per the fact sheet's own more specific count, "only 52 Non-government organizations registered under [LASPNET] involved in legal aid service provision," covering "only 70 districts ... out of 121 districts" — i.e., geographic coverage gaps are an acknowledged, named limitation.

**Scale of the access gap** (LASPNET fact sheet, citing a 2016 HiiL report): "**80% of Ugandans cannot afford services of lawyers, only 20% are able to engage a lawyer**," and separately, "**about 2600 lawyers are serving 34.6 million people** with their offices located in urban areas." **[Secondary — these are circa-2016/2017-vintage figures per internal dating clues in the source document (34.6 million matches Uganda's population around 2016-2017, well below Uganda's current ~48 million); treat as historically illustrative of the scale of the access gap, not as a current statistic.]**

**Product-design implication.** "Lawyers" as a Pleadli app cannot be scoped only to private-fee-paying-client advocates without excluding a very large, institutionally real, and now (as of the 2022 Rules) formally structured segment of practice: **advocates on the Roll of State Appointed Advocates**, who have court-facing procedural duties (client-meeting confirmations, certificates of trial readiness, monitoring feedback forms) that are distinct from an ordinary private-retainer relationship and that route through a Judiciary-administered Roll rather than a client engagement letter. An advocate could plausibly appear in the product as running **both kinds of matters simultaneously** — a paying civil/corporate client and a state-appointed capital-case defense — which argues for the product modeling "how this matter is funded/instructed" (private retainer vs. state-appointed vs. pro bono under the 2009 Regulations) as a first-class matter attribute rather than an afterthought.

## 5. Court-facing documents a defense lawyer actually produces

Drawing on the **Trial on Indictments Act, Chapter 25** (fetched directly from the Laws.Africa/ULII media host and read in full — the same source and method `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` used) for High Court criminal trial procedure: **[Primary]**

- **§73 "Close of case for the prosecution"**: once prosecution evidence and any committal-stage statement of the accused are complete, the court — "after hearing the advocates for the prosecution and for the defence" — either records a "not guilty" finding (functionally the "no case to answer" outcome, though the Act does not use that exact phrase, confirming `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md`'s own finding on this point) or informs the accused of the right to give evidence, make an unsworn statement, or call witnesses, "and shall then ask the accused person, **or his or her advocate**" whether these rights will be exercised.
- **§74 "Defence"**: "The accused person or his or her advocate may then open his or her case, stating the facts or law on which he or she intends to rely, and making such comments as he or she thinks necessary on the evidence for the prosecution" — this is the primary-source basis for what is informally called a **"defence case"/opening statement**; the Act does not name a specific written-document form for it (it is described as an oral procedural act: opening, examining witnesses, summing up), so a formal "Defence Case Summary" as a distinct filed document is **not confirmed by this primary text** — **UNKNOWN whether Ugandan practice produces a written defence case summary as a standard filed document**, as distinct from the oral procedural act the Act actually describes.
- **§75 "Witnesses for the defence"**: the accused may examine any witness present, but has no right to compel summons of witnesses beyond those already named to the committing magistrate's court.
- **§79 "Additional material facts"**: the prosecution must give the accused **"or his or her advocate"** written notice before alleging any fact not already in the summary of the case — a specific, primary-source-confirmed disclosure obligation running *to* the defence advocate.

**Bail applications.** Multiple secondary/academic sources (a Foundation for Human Rights Initiative-published "Bail Handbook," Uganda law-school course materials on "bail application procedure and practice") confirm bail applications are a standard, named defence-advocate activity, grounded in the Article 28(3)(a) presumption of innocence and governed procedurally by the Criminal Procedure Code Act and Magistrates Courts Act (the latter's ss.75-79 bail provisions were flagged as read only in secondary summary, not fetched in full, by `docs/police/PRODUCT.md`-adjacent research; this research did not independently re-fetch them either). **[Secondary]** No specific, named standard bail-application form was confirmed in this research — **UNKNOWN whether a numbered/standard bail-application form exists**, as distinct from a free-form advocate's motion.

**Mitigation submissions ("plea in mitigation").** The Trial on Indictments Act's own §108, "Mitigation of penalties" (read in full), is narrower than the practitioner phrase suggests: it is a **sentencing-discretion provision for the court** ("A person liable to imprisonment for life ... may be sentenced for any shorter term"), not a defined defence-side document. The practitioner concept of defence counsel making **"submissions in mitigation of sentence"** after conviction or guilty plea is well-attested in Ugandan case law and secondary commentary (multiple High Court judgments quote defence counsel's mitigation submissions directly) but is a **procedural practice, not itself a named statutory filing** with a prescribed form. **[Secondary for the practice itself; Primary — but narrower than expected — for the statutory hook in §108]**

**Bottom line**: defence counsel's court-facing role in Ugandan criminal procedure, per the primary TIA text, is substantially built around **oral advocacy at defined procedural moments** (the §73/§74 defence-case sequence, mitigation submissions, bail argument) rather than a suite of distinctly-named written filings comparable to the prosecution's Charge Sheet/Indictment/Summary of the Case (`docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §6). The one confirmed **written, defence-specific document with a prescribed statutory form** found in this research is not a generic "defence case summary" but the **plea agreement and certificate-of-trial-readiness documents described in §§3-4 above**, both of which are jointly authored/signed with the prosecution rather than defence-only artifacts.

## 6. Statistics on the profession — solo, small-firm, or larger-organization user

**Total advocate/membership numbers.** The Uganda Law Society's own 2023/2024 Annual Report (read in full) states: **[Primary]**
> "The number of ULS paid-up members has gradually increased over the last 5 years from **3239 members in 2019 to 4783 in 2023**."

Age/experience-band breakdown given in the same report (note: the report's own text and its accompanying graphic label are **internally inconsistent** on one figure, flagged rather than silently resolved): **[Primary, with a caveat]**
> "The ULS statistics currently stand at **3,263 young lawyers with 5 years and below of practice** (49%), **1,241 [lawyers] with 6-10 years of practice** (19%), and **2,136** lawyers described in the report's own body text as "10 years of practice and above" but labeled in the accompanying graphic as **"Senior lawyers (20 years of practice and above)"** (32%).

**This 10-years-vs-20-years discrepancy is a direct artifact of the source document itself, not an error introduced by this research — flagged explicitly so a future reader does not silently pick one figure over the other without checking the report directly.**

Two things follow from this breakdown: (a) **49% of paid-up ULS members have 5 years' practice or less** — a heavily junior-skewed profession by this measure, and (b) **no source found gives a comparable breakdown by firm size** (solo vs. small firm vs. large firm) — **UNKNOWN**, despite a specific search for this.

**Note on what "ULS paid-up members" does and does not measure.** Because ULS membership subscription payment is a Advocates Act §12(1)(e) precondition for holding a valid practising certificate (§1 above), "paid-up ULS members" is a reasonable proxy for "advocates currently entitled to practice," but it is **not necessarily identical to the total number of names ever entered on the Chief Justice's Roll of Advocates** (which would include those who have left practice, been struck off, or lapsed) — this distinction was not separately quantified by any source found. **UNKNOWN** what the raw "total ever enrolled" figure is, as distinct from the ~4,783 current-paid-up-member figure.

**Firm-size proxy evidence (indirect).** No official Law Council or ULS statistic on firm-size distribution was found. The best available proxy is that Uganda's own largest, most prominent law firm — **Kampala Associated Advocates (KAA)**, explicitly described (by its own promotional/Wikipedia-sourced material) as "one of the largest law firms in Uganda" — had **26 lawyers, 15 of them partners, as of 2020**. **[Secondary]** If Uganda's largest firm by reputation is a firm of ~26 lawyers, this strongly implies (though does not itself state) that the *typical* Ugandan law firm is materially smaller still — likely solo practitioners or small partnerships of a handful of advocates — but this is an **inference from firm-size-at-the-top**, not a direct statistic on the median or modal firm size. **Treat as suggestive, not confirmed.**

**Access-gap statistics** (already cited in §4, repeated here for the specific product-sizing question): "~2,600 lawyers serving 34.6 million people" (a circa-2016/2017 LASPNET/HiiL-sourced figure, now stale relative to Uganda's current population but directionally telling) and "80% of Ugandans cannot afford ... lawyers." **[Secondary, dated]**

**No ULS- or Law Council-published breakdown by practice-area/case-type was found** (e.g., what proportion of the profession's work is criminal defense vs. civil vs. corporate/conveyancing) — **UNKNOWN**, beyond the qualitative "clusters" evidence in §2 (Family Law, IP, Tax named; no dedicated criminal cluster found).

**Bottom line for product sizing**: the concrete, primary-sourced membership figure (~4,783 paid-up ULS members, 2023, heavily junior-skewed) combined with the indirect firm-size evidence (Uganda's *largest* firm being ~26 lawyers) both point toward **solo practitioners and small firms being the realistic default assumption** for a Pleadli Lawyers user, with larger commercial firms (KAA-scale, dozens of lawyers) as a real but numerically minority segment, and with criminal-defense work specifically (per §2's findings) more likely concentrated among smaller/solo/general-litigation practitioners — including advocates juggling private civil/corporate retainers alongside state-appointed or pro bono criminal defense work under the schemes described in §4 — rather than among the largest commercial firms. This composite conclusion is **this research's own synthesis of several partial, independently-sourced data points**, not a single source's stated conclusion, and should be weighted accordingly.

## Central question: where does Plea Bargaining belong?

Drawing together §§3-4 above, the evidence points toward **plea bargaining being a genuinely cross-institutional process that neither a DPP-only nor a Lawyers-only product design correctly represents on its own**, for four independent, converging reasons found in this research:

1. **The plea agreement's own prescribed form (Rule 9, Schedule 1) is structurally co-authored** — it contains dedicated fields for both the prosecuting agent's and the accused's advocate's supporting statements, and is signed by the prosecutor, the accused, *and* the accused's advocate (where represented). It is not analogous to the Charge Sheet or Indictment (`docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §6), which are unilaterally DPP-authored documents the defense merely responds to in court.
2. **A separate confirmation document (Rule 12(5), Schedule 3) is signed by "the parties" before the presiding judicial officer** and becomes part of the court record — introducing a third institutional actor (the court/Judiciary) into the paper trail as well, not just prosecution and defense.
3. **Ugandan case law explicitly frames the negotiation as three-party**, not two-party: *Lwanga Suleman v. Uganda* (2019) describes the ideal timing as enabling "**the state, the accused and defence counsel**" to jointly agree on amending the charge — defence counsel is named as a co-equal negotiating party to the process by the courts themselves, not merely a witness to a prosecutor-accused negotiation.
4. **The explanation-of-agreement duty (before the accused signs) is assigned to "the advocate or a justice of the peace"** — a duty that can fall to either a defense lawyer or a judicial officer, but is conspicuously *not* assigned to the prosecutor. This is a specific structural signal that the Rules treat "making sure the accused actually understands and freely consents" as belonging on the defense/judicial side of the table, deliberately separate from the prosecutor who is the accused's negotiating counterparty.

At the same time, this research also found (per `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §§4/6-7) that **ODPP has its own dedicated institutional home for this process — a named "Plea Bargain Unit" within its Directorate of Prosecutions** — meaning ODPP already treats plea bargaining as core, owned work with its own internal unit, guidelines chapter, and case-management expectations (PROCAM/PROCAMIS recording). Nothing found in this research suggests Uganda's defense bar has an equivalent dedicated institutional structure for plea bargaining specifically (no "Plea Bargain" desk or cluster was found within ULS, the Law Council, or the state-legal-representation scheme) — defense-side plea bargaining appears to be handled by whichever advocate already has conduct of the matter (private, state-appointed, or pro bono), as one part of ordinary case handling, not as a specialized defense-side function analogous to ODPP's unit.

**Best-evidenced answer**: this is genuinely a **shared, cross-institutional process by the actual documented structure of the plea agreement itself** — not a DPP-owned process the defense merely participates in, and not something that should be modeled as exclusively a Lawyers-app workflow either, since a Lawyers-only model would miss that a Judiciary-facing confirmation step and an ODPP-side dedicated unit/case-tracking system both sit alongside the defense advocate's participation. If Pleadli's product architecture requires each app to "own" a given workflow rather than duplicate it across apps, plea bargaining is a strong candidate for being modeled as a **shared record type both DPP's and Lawyers' apps can see/act on their own side of** (mirroring how court case numbers, per `docs/dpp/RESEARCH.md` §5, already have to be tracked by ODPP independently of a live system-to-system feed from the Judiciary) — rather than a record that lives natively inside only one app's data model. This is a product-design recommendation drawn from the evidence, not itself something any source states; the underlying legal/procedural facts it rests on (co-signature structure, three-party case-law framing, ODPP's dedicated unit, absence of an equivalent defense-side dedicated unit) are the load-bearing, source-grounded claims.

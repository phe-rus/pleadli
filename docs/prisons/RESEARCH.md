# Uganda Prisons Service Research Brief

Research conducted to ground the design of "Pleadli Prisons," a purpose-built app for the Uganda Prisons Service (UPS), sibling to Pleadli Police and Pleadli DPP. This is the first foundational research pass for the Prisons side. Domains are established from UPS's own mandate and statute rather than assumed to mirror Police or DPP. Structured by the 8 question areas in the research brief, plus a References section.

**Confidence tags used throughout:** **[Primary]** = verified against the actual constitutional/statutory/official-document text fetched and read in full during this research. **[Secondary]** = drawn from a named secondary source (news, NGO, academic aggregator, or non-UPS government page) because a primary source could not be located or did not establish the detail in full. **UNKNOWN** = not established by any source found; stated plainly rather than guessed.

## 1. Legal mandate & structure

**Constitutional mandate.** The Constitution of the Republic of Uganda, 1995 (text as consolidated to 5 January 2018, fetched and read in full) establishes the Uganda Prisons Service in three articles: **[Primary]**

> **215. Uganda Prisons Service**
> (1) There shall be a prisons service to be known as the Uganda Prisons Service.
> (2) The Uganda Prisons Service shall be nationalistic, patriotic, professional, disciplined, competent and productive; and its members shall be citizens of Uganda of good character recruited from every district of Uganda.
>
> **216. Commissioner and Deputy Commissioner of Prisons**
> (1) There shall be a Commissioner of Prisons and a Deputy Commissioner of Prisons appointed by the President with the approval of Parliament.
> (2) The Commissioner or the Deputy Commissioner of Prisons may be removed by the President.
>
> **217. Parliament to regulate the Uganda Prisons Service**
> Parliament shall make laws — (a) providing for the organisation, administration and functions of the Uganda Prisons Service; (b) ensuring that members of the Uganda Prisons Service are recruited from every district of Uganda; and (c) regulating generally the Uganda Prisons Service.

Note the Constitution itself names the top office **"Commissioner of Prisons,"** not "Commissioner General" — the "General" suffix is a statutory/practice addition (see below), a minor but real terminology gap between the constitutional text and the Act/current usage.

**Statutory basis.** Pursuant to Article 217, Parliament enacted the **Prisons Act, 2006** (Act 17 of 2006, assented 24 May 2006, commenced 14 July 2006), which repealed and replaced the colonial-era Prisons Act, Cap. 304. Its long title states its purpose: "to repeal and replace the Prisons Act, Cap. 304 in order to bring it in line with the Constitution, to establish the Prisons Authority and the Prisons Council; to bring Local Administration Prisons under the Uganda Prisons Service; to bring the Act in line with effective and humane modern penal policy and universally accepted international standards." **[Primary — full text fetched and read, 125 sections across 11 Parts plus two schedules]**

**Objective (Prisons Act s.4).** "The main objective of the Service is to contribute to the protection of all members of society by providing reasonable, safe, secure and humane custody and rehabilitation of offenders in accordance with universally accepted standards." This matches, almost verbatim, UPS's own current public mission statement ("To contribute to the protection and development of society by providing safe, secure, and humane custody of prisoners while placing human rights at the centre of their correctional programmes") — confirming the statutory objective is still the institution's live self-description. **[Primary]**

**Functions (s.5):** to keep every legally-detained person in humane, safe custody and produce them in court when required until lawfully discharged or removed; facilitate social rehabilitation/reformation through training and education; facilitate reintegration into communities; ensure prisoners perform work reasonably necessary for prison management; and any other function the Minister assigns after consulting the Prisons Authority. **[Primary]**

**Governance bodies.** The Act establishes two national bodies plus regional/district committees: **[Primary]**

- **Prisons Authority** (s.9-12) — chaired by the Minister responsible for internal affairs; members include the Attorney General (or representative), the Commissioner General, the Deputy Commissioner General, the Permanent Secretary of the ministry responsible for public service, a senior officer-in-charge of personnel, and two presidential appointees (2-year renewable terms). Quorum 5; meets at least every 4 months. Functions: advises Government on policy; advises the President on appointing the Commissioner General/Deputy; **appoints and promotes officers of and above the rank of Assistant Superintendent of Prisons but below Deputy Commissioner General**; sets strategic direction, terms/conditions of service; hears appeals from Prisons Council decisions; makes rules on prison management, prisoner welfare, and officer conduct.
- **Prisons Council** (s.13-15) — chaired by the Commissioner General, vice-chaired by the Deputy Commissioner General; members include Directors, heads of headquarters units (prisoners' administration, training, finance, personnel, legal affairs, research/planning, welfare/rehabilitation), all Regional Prisons Commanders, plus appointed representatives at Assistant Superintendent, Principal Officer, and three non-commissioned-officer levels. Quorum 10; meets at least every 3 months. Functions: **appoints/promotes officers up to Principal Officer rank**; exercises disciplinary control over officers at/below Principal Officer; advises the Authority on rank structure; sets recruitment/training standards and equipment/supply standards.
- **Regional Prisons Committees** (s.16, one per region) and **District Prisons Committees** (s.17, one per district) — advisory bodies on appointments/promotions/personnel matters, chaired respectively by the Regional Prisons Commander and District Prisons Commander.

**Rank hierarchy — confirmed against UPS's own current data.** UPS's own **Monthly Statistics Summary for March 2025** (fetched and extracted in full from the PDF hosted on ugandaprisons.go.ug) includes a literal staff-by-rank table, "Uniformed staff by Rank," which is the single most authoritative source found for the full descending rank order. **Administrative structure below rank of Commissioner (Prisons Act Part IV, ss.27-35):** **[Primary]**
- **Officer in charge** (s.30) — appointed by the Commissioner General for each individual prison; responsible for stores/valuables accountability, administration, directives, record-keeping, and is answerable to the Commissioner General through the Regional Prisons Commander and Deputy Commissioner General.
- **Regional Prisons Commander** (s.32-33) — a senior officer heading each region, vested with administration/supervision of all prisons in that region, responsible for implementing departmental policy and coordinating institutional activities in the region.
- **District Prisons Commander** (s.34, named in the s.17 committee composition) — district-level counterpart, with a "Deputy Prisons Commander" as deputy at district level.

**How many prisons/stations exist nationally.** UPS's own **"Prisoners and Staff Statistics Report for January, 2024"** (a 40-page PDF, fetched and extracted in full) states explicitly, twice: "**266 custodial prison units countrywide**," and that "the number of prisoners per custodial staff ranged between four (4) and nine (09) across the 266 custodial units." **[Primary]** The independent academic/NGO aggregator **World Prison Brief** (prisonstudies.org, data as of its most recent Uganda profile update) separately reports **270 establishments** for Uganda, sourced from Uganda Prisons Service data — the small difference from 266 is most likely just a different snapshot date (institutions open/close over time) rather than a contradiction. **[Secondary, but itself sourced from UPS]**

**Regional structure.** The same January 2024 report's regional population table (Table 1.2) names, and gives population/congestion data for, **19 distinct UPS regions**: Southern, Northern, Kigezi, Mid Central, Central, Kampala Extra, Eastern, North Central, Mid Western, Mid Northern, Mid Eastern, South Western, East Central, North Eastern, Iganga, Western, North Western, Kooki, South Eastern. **[Primary]** This is a genuine, citable parallel to the ODPP's "19 regions / 127 stations" structure noted in the sibling DPP research — UPS also runs a 19-region model, though over 266 (or ~270) individual prison stations rather than ODPP's 127. Region names follow historical/cultural sub-regional names (e.g. Kigezi, Kooki, Iganga) rather than simple compass points, unlike a naive "Central/Northern/Eastern/Western" assumption.

**Staff establishment (approved vs. filled).** Wikipedia's Uganda Prisons Service article states Cabinet approved a staff structure of **49,470 total staff** (37,631 uniformed, 11,839 non-uniformed) in 2016, across 6 directorates with 23 departments — this figure is independently corroborated inside UPS's own August 2024 internal staff-structure review document, which states "Cabinet approved the Uganda Prisons Staff Structure with an approved staff establishment of 49,470 staff... currently, this structure is filled up to 29.1%," and that the 2024 review proposed adjusting the approved total slightly to 49,448 while raising actual staff-in-post to 14,382. **[Primary — UPS's own document confirms the Wikipedia-cited figure]** The March 2025 monthly report's total headcount (13,941) and the January 2024 report's (14,261) are both in the same range, consistent with an establishment still well under half-filled.

## 2. Core record terminology

**Statutory definitions (Prisons Act s.2, "Interpretation")**, quoted directly: **[Primary]**

- **"prisoner"** means "a person duly committed to custody under the writ, warrant or order of a court exercising criminal jurisdiction or by order of a court-martial."
- **"convicted prisoner"** means "a prisoner under a sentence of a court or court-martial."
- **"appellant prisoner"** means "a convicted prisoner who is detained in a prison as a result of a conviction which is the subject matter of an appeal, notice of which has been accepted but the decision in regard to which has not been given" (with a specific carve-out for prisoners who elected under Criminal Procedure Code Act s.40(5)).
- **"civil prisoner"** means "an indebted person entitled to special treatment" — i.e. a debtor imprisoned for civil debt, a distinct third category from criminal prisoners (see the "Debtors" column in UPS's own population statistics, §8 below).
- **"officer in charge"**, **"prison officer"**, **"senior prison officer"**, **"junior prison officer"**, **"Commissioner General"**, **"Deputy Commissioner General"**, **"medical officer"**, **"lock-up"** are all separately defined (see relevant sections below).

**"Remand" is not a defined noun in the Act.** The Act never defines a term "remand prisoner" as such; instead it uses **"unconvicted prisoner(s)"** as the operative category (s.64, "Custody of unconvicted prisoners": "Prisoners who are not convicted are presumed to be innocent and shall be treated as such and they shall be kept separate from convicted prisoners") and describes the act of remand as a verb/process ("committed for remand in a prison by order of a court," s.64(2)). **[Primary]** In UPS's own published statistics, however, **"Remands"** (plural noun) is the standing column header used alongside "Convicts" and "Debtors" (see the March 2025 and January 2024 statistics reports, §8 below) — so in day-to-day operational/statistical usage "remand" functions as UPS's own noun for this population, even though the Act's drafting uses "unconvicted prisoner." **[Primary, from UPS's own statistical publications]**

**"Inmate" vs "prisoner."** The Act itself uses **"prisoner"** and **"prison officer"** exclusively — "inmate" does not appear in the Prisons Act 2006's substantive text. However, government communications about UPS do use "inmate" informally and interchangeably: the Ministry of Finance's Ministerial Policy Statement for Vote 145 (Uganda Prisons) reports rehabilitation achievements in terms of "inmates" ("3,131 inmates on formal educational programs," "12,562 inmates undergoing skills training"). **[Primary — MoF budget document]** Bottom line: **"prisoner" is the Act's and UPS's own statistical-reporting noun of record; "inmate" is used informally in government prose (especially rehabilitation contexts) but has no statutory definition.**

**No official prisoner reference/registration number format was found.** Section 61 ("Particulars of prisoners to be recorded") states only: "Upon admission of any person into custody, the officer in charge shall record or cause to be recorded in the relevant registers particulars of that person" — it does not name a specific number format, nor does any other section of the Act. **UNKNOWN** — unlike ODPP's named "ODPP registration number," no equivalent named prisoner-number scheme (e.g. a "PRN" or similar) was found in the Prisons Act or on UPS's public site. UPS's own internal documents do name an information system relevant to this: the Ministry of Finance's Vote 145 policy statement (reporting on FY2016/17) mentions "development of Prisoners Management Information Systems (PMIS)... ongoing," and UPS's own August 2024 staff-structure review separately refers to a "Prisons Information Management System" division (since renamed/merged into a "Statistics division" under the Planning department, and into a "Technology and ICT Innovations division"). **[Primary, both from UPS/MoF documents]** These confirm a UPS-run information system exists (by two overlapping names, PMIS / Prisons Information Management System) but neither source describes its prisoner-numbering scheme in enough detail to state a format — **UNKNOWN**.

## 3. The remand vs. convicted distinction, operationally

**Presumption of innocence and physical separation (s.64):** "Prisoners who are not convicted are presumed to be innocent and shall be treated as such and they shall be kept separate from convicted prisoners." Where a prisoner is committed for remand "by order of a court or other competent authority," the officer in charge receives the prisoner together with **"the warrant of commitment"** and must detain the prisoner "for a period indicated in the warrant" and discharge "at such time, or as to the terms of the warrant in question may specify." **[Primary]** Section 65 adds a special unconvicted-status rule: a prisoner who elects to postpone serving a sentence pending High Court confirmation is treated as unconvicted between the election and the confirmation/order, and that time still counts toward the eventual sentence if confirmed.

**Legal-aid/counsel access for untried prisoners (s.64(3)):** an untried prisoner may apply for free legal aid where available, receive legal-advisor visits, and be supplied writing materials, with prisoner-lawyer interviews conducted "within sight but not within the hearing of a police or prison officer." **[Primary]**

**What Prisons tracks about a remand prisoner's case status — the clearest statutory mechanism found is s.118, "List of prisoners awaiting trial":** **[Primary]**

> "The officer in charge of a prison shall, before the commencement of a session of the High Court, file in that court a list of every prisoner within that prison awaiting trial in that court, the date of his or her admission into prison and the authority for his or her detention and when last appeared in court."

This is a real, named, statutory obligation that directly answers the design question of what a remand-tracking view needs to hold per prisoner: **admission date, the authority for detention (i.e. the warrant/order), and date last appeared in court** — the exact three fields a "next court date"/remand-review feature would need to reconstruct or extend. No separate field for "next court date" itself is named in the Act — that would need to be derived/tracked operationally rather than being a named statutory data point.

**Production of a remand prisoner to court — the mechanism exists but has no specific named form.** Section 67, "Prisoners required as witnesses," provides: **[Primary]**

> "Whenever the presence of a person confined in a prison is required by a court or other competent authority, the court or competent authority may issue an order addressed to the officer in charge requiring production before the court or other competent authority of the person in proper custody at a time and place to be named in the order... A prisoner taken from a prison in pursuance of an order made under this section shall, while outside the prison, be kept in custody as the officer in charge may direct and while in custody shall be deemed to be in lawful custody."

This is Prisons' formal court-production mechanism — an **"order"** issued by the court to the officer in charge, endorsable for repeat production if the matter is adjourned (s.67(3)). The Act does not give this order a specific named form (unlike, say, DPP's "Police Form 53" charge sheet) — **UNKNOWN** whether a standard numbered production-warrant form exists in practice; the January 2024 UPS statistics report separately confirms the scale of this activity in raw numbers ("Number of prisoners produced to courts were 36,239 [in a month] — a daily average of 1,726 prisoners") without naming a specific form. **[Primary for the numbers, UNKNOWN for a specific form name/number]**

**Remand-period limits — a real, citable conflict between two primary legal texts.** Both the Constitution and the Prisons Act state numeric remand-to-mandatory-bail thresholds, and **they do not match**:

- **Constitution Article 23(6)(b)-(c)** (as amended — both clauses were "substituted by section 9 of Act 11 of 2005," per the annotated consolidated text): **[Primary — verbatim, fetched and read in full]**
  > "(b) in the case of an offence which is triable by the High Court as well as by a subordinate court, if that person has been remanded in custody in respect of the offence for **sixty days** before trial, that person shall be released on bail...
  > (c) in the case of an offence triable only by the High Court, if that person has been remanded in custody for **one hundred and eighty days** before the case is committed to the High Court, that person shall be released on bail..."
- **Prisons Act, 2006, s.66, "Release on bail"** (enacted the year after that 2005 constitutional amendment, yet not matching it): **[Primary — verbatim]**
  > "(a) in the case of an offence which is triable by the High Court as well as by a subordinate court, the person shall be released on bail... if that person has been remanded in custody in respect of the offence before trial for **one hundred and twenty days**;
  > (b) in the case of an offence triable only by the High Court, the person shall be released on bail... if the person has been remanded in custody for **three hundred and sixty days** before the case is committed to the High Court."

The Constitution is the supreme law of Uganda ("This Constitution is the supreme law of Uganda and shall have binding force on all authorities and persons throughout Uganda... If any other law or any custom is inconsistent with any of the provisions of this Constitution, the Constitution shall prevail, and that other law or custom shall, to the extent of the inconsistency, be void" — Article 2(1)-(2), **[Primary]**), so the constitutionally-controlling figures should be **60 days** (hybrid-jurisdiction offences) and **180 days** (High-Court-only offences). The Prisons Act's 120-day/360-day figures appear to be a statute-book drafting artifact that was never harmonised with the 2005 constitutional amendment, despite the Act being passed a year later. **A Pleadli Prisons design should use the constitutional 60/180-day figures for any mandatory-bail-eligibility flag, and should not rely on the Prisons Act's 120/360-day text**, though this is a genuine legal ambiguity worth flagging to a domain expert rather than silently resolving. Secondary reporting on Ugandan case law is consistent with the Constitution's 180-day figure being the one actually applied in practice for High-Court-only offences today (a High Court ruling reported by a Uganda legal-news outlet describes a judge "clarify[ing] that courts cannot deny mandatory bail based on the gravity of the offence" under "the 180-Day Mandatory Bail Rule"). **[Secondary — Lawpoint Uganda]**

Separately, Article 23(6) does **not** carve out a distinct "capital offence" category — capital offences (murder, treason, aggravated robbery, aggravated defilement, etc.) are simply a subset of "offences triable only by the High Court" and fall under clause (c)'s 180-day threshold; there is no separate constitutional clause specifically naming "capital offence" for remand-limit purposes. **[Primary]**

**Cross-reference to the police-side 48-hour rule, confirmed inside the Prisons Act itself (s.117):** "A police officer or any other law enforcement officer may arrest and detain an accused person in a lock-up pending trial, but in any case for not more than forty-eight hours from the time of his or her arrest," and "a lock-up shall not be a prison for the purposes of this Act." **[Primary]** This corroborates, from the Prisons side, the same 48-hour rule the sibling Police research would have found from Article 23(4) of the Constitution.

## 4. Admission & release process

**What triggers admission — and how it is documented (s.58):** **[Primary — verbatim]**

> "(1) No person shall be received or admitted into prison custody without a valid **commitment or a remand warrant, order of detention, warrant of conviction or committal** signed with a court seal or authenticated by a person authorised to sign or authenticate such warrant or order under the provision of any law."

So Ugandan law names (at least) four distinct instrument types that can trigger admission: a **commitment**, a **remand warrant**, an **order of detention**, or a **warrant of conviction/committal**. The officer in charge has an affirmative duty to verify, before admission, that "the particulars of each person are accurately described in the warrant or order of detention accompanying such person" and that "such warrant or order of detention has been properly issued or authenticated" (s.58(2)). A person may not be admitted if "severely tortured and in a bad health condition" (s.58(3)) — a welfare-driven admission refusal that is distinct from a purely paperwork-driven one. A clerical error on the warrant's face is **not** grounds to refuse admission outright — the officer must instead take steps to have the error rectified as soon as practicable (s.58(4)). A juvenile may never be admitted into an adult-designated prison (s.58(7)). Where a sentenced person is temporarily held in a police cell/lock-up under the Commissioner General's authority (e.g. no prison established locally), that detention is capped at one month, extendable only by the Commissioner General (s.58(6)).

**Particulars recorded on admission (s.61):** "Upon admission of any person into custody, the officer in charge shall record or cause to be recorded in the relevant registers particulars of that person" — the Act does not itself enumerate which particulars, leaving that to rules/practice. **UNKNOWN** exact prescribed particulars/fields.

**What triggers release.** The Act does not centralise "release" into one section listing every trigger; release events are scattered and mostly court-driven or administrative:

- **Bail** — s.66, subject to the 120-day/360-day (Act) vs. 60-day/180-day (Constitution) conflict discussed in §3. **[Primary]**
- **Full remission** — s.83: "The officer in charge shall be responsible for the due discharge of a prisoner immediately upon becoming entitled to release," discharged "before noon on the date on which he or she is entitled to be released" (or the preceding weekday if that date is a Sunday/public holiday). A prisoner still under medical treatment on their release date is instead discharged "to a medical or social service outside the prison as necessary." **[Primary]**
- **Partial remission** — s.84: a convicted prisoner serving over one month "may by industry and good conduct earn a remission of one third of his or her sentence," credited in full at admission and forfeitable for prison-discipline offences (s.85). **[Primary]**
- **Parole** — s.89: a prisoner serving 3+ years may, within 6 months of their release date, be allowed by the Commissioner General onto parole for up to 3 months; failure to return or a parole-condition breach is itself an offence, punishable as if the prisoner had escaped. **[Primary]**
- **Release on licence** — s.87, mandatory for "habitual criminals" (defined in s.87(13) as sentenced on four separate occasions for offences in Penal Code Act Chapters XXVI-XXXIX) released under s.83; licence can be revoked, triggering a magistrate's re-committal warrant for the unexpired sentence portion. **[Primary]**
- **Acquittal / court-ordered discharge** — the Act assumes this is driven entirely by the court's own order reaching the officer in charge (s.63(2): "The officer in charge shall ensure that every person committed to prison under a warrant or order, is detained in accordance with the provisions of the warrant or order, until the person is lawfully released from prison") rather than separately defining "acquittal" as a Prisons-side event. **[Primary]**
- **Erroneous release / re-arrest (s.79):** if the Commissioner General "reasonably suspects that a prisoner has been released from a prison erroneously," he/she may issue a re-arrest warrant executable by any police or prison officer, which then authorises re-detention. **[Primary]** — a real, named correction mechanism for a release-in-error.

**Remand-period limits:** covered fully in §3 above (Article 23(6)(b)-(c) vs. Prisons Act s.66 — 60/180 days constitutionally, 120/360 days per the unreformed Act text).

**Escape.** A prisoner who escapes while being transferred/conveyed between prisons is expressly "deemed to have escaped from lawful custody" (s.73(6)). The Commissioner General may offer a monetary reward for information leading to an escapee's apprehension (s.119), though prison officers and police/security personnel are generally excluded from claiming that reward absent "exceptional circumstances" in the Commissioner General's opinion. **[Primary]** The Act does **not** contain an explicit "the officer in charge shall notify [police/DPP/court] within X hours of an escape" clause — **UNKNOWN** whether such a notification duty exists in unpublished Standing Orders or rules; only the general re-arrest/reward machinery is stated in the Act itself.

## 5. Health/welfare records

**Statutory basis for prison health services (s.26, "Appointment, powers and duties of medical officer"):** **[Primary — verbatim]**

> "(1) The Prisons Authority may, in consultation with the Health Service Commission, appoint a Director of Health Services, who will be responsible for direction and control of all health services in the entire Service.
> (2) The Prisons Authority may, in consultation with the Health Service Commission, appoint qualified medical officers at every prison in the country who shall be responsible for all health care of all prisoners and staff in the prison for which he or she has been appointed.
> (3) The functions and powers of the medical officer shall be prescribed by rules."

Critically, the Act's own **definition** of "medical officer" (s.2) shows this role is **not necessarily a dedicated UPS employee**: "'medical officer' means either the district director of health or a medical officer in the district or area in which the prison is situated or, in his or her absence, any registered or licensed Government medical practitioner or the medical officer appointed to a prison **if a medical officer has not been appointed**." **[Primary]** This directly answers the "Prisons-owned vs. separate health authority" question: **the Act deliberately leaves it hybrid** — the Prisons Authority *may* appoint dedicated prison medical officers (in consultation with the Health Service Commission, the Ministry-of-Health-linked body), but the statutory fallback, where no dedicated appointment has been made, is simply the district (Ministry of Health) health officer covering that area. There is no single, exclusively-Prisons-owned health service mandated by the Act; it is designed to interoperate with the general public health service structure.

**No named "health register" or standardised medical-record requirement was found** in the Act's text — the general "registers" language in ss.61-62 covers admission particulars and property, not health records specifically. **UNKNOWN** whether a standardised prisoner health-record format is mandated by rules made under s.26(3) (those rules were not located in this research pass).

**Prisoner rights and hospital-transfer provisions relevant to health:**
- s.57(f): every prisoner is entitled to "access to the health services available in the country without discrimination of their legal situation." **[Primary]**
- s.74: insane/mentally-abnormal prisoners "shall not be detained in prison" — they must be moved to a mental hospital under the Mental Treatment Act as soon as possible, on a medical officer's or officer-in-charge's opinion, confirmed by a magistrate; time in the mental hospital counts toward the sentence. **[Primary]**
- ss.75-76: sick prisoners may be moved to a hospital on the medical officer's advice (or, in an emergency, by the officer in charge alone); the prisoner is deemed still "under detention in the prison from which he or she was so removed," and the hospital-side medical officer must notify the sending prison's officer in charge once the prisoner no longer needs hospital care. **[Primary]**

**Referral hospital.** **Murchison Bay Hospital**, located on the grounds of Luzira Maximum Security Prison (Nakawa, Kampala), operates as UPS's national referral hospital for prisoners, treating HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis and mental-health conditions among the prison population, with complex cases referred onward to Mulago National Referral Hospital. **[Secondary — Wikipedia, not independently cross-checked against a UPS primary source in this research pass]**

**Health as an overcrowding-adjacent metric UPS itself tracks:** UPS's own Ministry-of-Finance-reported figures include a prisoner **mortality rate** ("maintained at 0.75 per 1,000 prisoners" for FY2016/17) and, per the January 2024 report, "mortality rate stood at 2.0 per 1,000 prisoners" for the July 2023-January 2024 period — both cited fully in §8 below with dates, since the two figures are nearly a decade apart and should not be conflated. **[Primary]**

## 6. Transfers between institutions

**General transfer power (s.73, "Removal of prisoners to other prisons"):** **[Primary — verbatim in relevant part]**

> "(1) A prisoner on being sentenced or during confinement, may be removed to any prison established under this Act.
> (2) A sentence of imprisonment lawfully imposed upon a person may be served partly in one prison and partly in another.
> (3) The Commissioner General may, by general or special order, direct that a prisoner be transferred from the prison to which he or she was committed or in which he or she is detained to another prison.
> (4) At the Commissioner General's discretion, a prisoner shall, if practicable be kept in and released from a prison situated in the area to which the prisoner belongs.
> (5) A prisoner who is being transferred or conveyed from one prison to another prison or place shall, while outside the prison, be deemed to be in the custody of the prison officer.
> (6) A prisoner who escapes from the custody of a prison officer, referred to in subsection (5) shall be deemed to have escaped from lawful custody."

This directly covers the remand-facility-to-post-conviction-prison scenario the research brief asked about: transfer authority rests with the Commissioner General (general or special order), and there is an explicit (if discretionary, "if practicable") preference for keeping/releasing a prisoner in the area they belong to.

**A specifically named transfer document does exist — the "transfer letter."** Section 78(2) ("Contact with outside world"): "On admission a prisoner shall be entitled to write and receive a 'reception letter' and to receive a visit and **on transfer to another prison a prisoner shall be entitled to write and receive a 'transfer letter.'**" **[Primary]** This is the one concretely named artifact tied specifically to a transfer event in the Act — a "transfer letter," paired conceptually with the admission-time "reception letter." Whether this is a communications artifact (prisoner-to-family notice) or also functions as an internal transfer *record* is not specified — the Act frames it as a prisoner-communication right, not explicitly an inter-prison paper trail.

**No separate, uniquely-named "transfer register" was found** distinct from the general record-keeping duty already imposed on every officer in charge (s.30(2)(d): "keep records in respect of the prison as may be required by the Commissioner General"). **UNKNOWN** whether a standardised national transfer-record form/register exists; only the Commissioner General's transfer-order power and the prisoner's "transfer letter" entitlement are named in the Act itself.

**Overcrowding-driven and emergency transfers (s.28, "Temporary prisons"):** the Commissioner General, with the Minister's approval, may direct temporary shelter/safe custody in a temporary prison when a prison's population "is greater than can be conveniently kept there and it is not convenient to transfer the excess number to some other prison," or due to an epidemic or other reason requiring temporary shelter. **[Primary]** This is a distinct, congestion-specific transfer/relocation power alongside the general s.73 transfer power.

## 7. Relationship to DPP/Courts

**Prisons-to-Courts: a formal, named reporting duty exists (s.118, already quoted in §3) — the pre-session "list of prisoners awaiting trial,"** filed by the officer in charge with the High Court before each session. **[Primary]** This is the clearest affirmative "Prisons must proactively tell the court something" duty found in the Act.

**Prisons-to-Courts: the production-order mechanism (s.67, already quoted in §3)** is reactive rather than proactive — the court/competent authority initiates it by issuing an order to the officer in charge, not the other way around. **[Primary]**

**No general "Prisons must notify DPP" statute analogous to a police-to-DPP referral duty was found.** The Prisons Act does not contain a provision requiring the officer in charge (or Commissioner General) to notify the DPP directly about a prisoner's escape, illness, or general status change. **UNKNOWN** whether such a duty exists in UPS Standing Orders (not located in this research pass) or in unpublished internal circulars.

**Death in custody — a real, statutory, multi-step notification chain exists, but it runs through the coroner and police, not directly to the DPP.** The **Inquests Act, Chapter 13** (fetched and read in full from the current consolidated text) is squarely on point: **[Primary]**

- **s.4(2)(a):** "Notwithstanding subsection (1), in the following cases the coroner shall always hold an inquest — (a) if the deceased died in prison or in police custody or while confined in any lockup or mental home." Death in prison is therefore one of a short, mandatory list of automatic-inquest triggers (alongside road-traffic deaths, workplace/mine-accident deaths, High-Court-directed inquests, and any Minister-declared class of case).
- **s.9, "Notice of death":** any person who finds a body or becomes aware of a death in suspicious circumstances "shall immediately inform the nearest LC I chairperson or officer in charge of a police station"; the LC I chairperson must then inform the nearest officer in charge of a police station, and "every officer in charge of a police station on receiving information shall inform a coroner having jurisdiction." Failure to perform this duty without good cause is itself an offence.
- **s.10(4):** where the death occurred in prison/police custody/a lockup/mental home **and** a medical practitioner is satisfied it was natural and unaccelerated by violence or negligence, the normal investigate-and-report chain (s.10(1)-(3)) is bypassed, and instead "the medical practitioner shall complete Form B in Schedule 2 to this Act and forward it to the coroner" directly — a simplified, medically-gated fast path specifically carved out for in-custody natural deaths.
- **DPP involvement is indirect, not automatic or first-line.** Section 24 ("Where guilty party unknown") provides that if the coroner suspects an offence but cannot identify the person responsible, the coroner "shall certify his or her opinion to that effect and transmit a copy of the proceedings to the **Inspector General of Police**" — not to the DPP. Only if the Inspector General of Police subsequently concludes "there is no probability that that person will be discovered" does the IGP then "transmit the copy of the proceedings to the **Director of Public Prosecutions**" (s.25). Separately, s.21(2) provides that where an inquest was stayed pending a related criminal trial and the coroner ultimately decides no public benefit would result from resuming it, the coroner "shall certify his or her opinion to that effect and transmit to the Director of Public Prosecutions a copy of the inquest proceedings" directly. So there are exactly two statutory DPP-notification paths for a death-in-custody inquest — one via the IGP (s.24-25, unidentified suspect) and one direct from the coroner (s.21(2), a stayed inquest not worth resuming) — and **neither originates from the Prisons Service itself**; Prisons' own statutory role in this chain is limited to being the location where the death occurred and (per s.9) a potential first "person...aware of the death" who must alert the LC I/police, not the coroner or DPP directly.

**Escape or health-issue notification to DPP/Courts specifically:** no statutory provision requiring this was found. **UNKNOWN.**

## 8. Official statistics

UPS publishes granular, dated **Monthly Statistics Summary / Monthly Statistical Abstract** reports on its own website (ugandaprisons.go.ug), at minimum from mid-2022 through at least December 2025 (visible in the site's own report archive). Two were fetched and read in full for this research: **[Primary throughout this section unless marked]**

**January 2024 report** ("Prisoners and Staff Statistics Report for January, 2024," 40 pages):
- Daily average prisoner population: **77,181** (up 0.3% from 76,943 in December 2023).
- Composition: **remands 47.9%** (36,936), **convicts 51.5%** (39,765), **debtors 0.6%** (480).
- **Occupancy rate: 367.6%** (up from 366.5% the prior month).
- **2,562** convicted prisoners normally released after completing sentence that month (daily average 102).
- **36,239** prisoners produced to courts that month — a **daily average of 1,726** productions.
- **10,743** newly admitted from court that month; **8,078** released from courts "due to various reasons."
- Escape rate (July 2023-January 2024 period): **3.1 escapees per 1,000 prisoners**; recapture rate **45.3%**; mortality rate **2.0 per 1,000 prisoners**.
- Staff strength: **14,261** (13,753 uniformed, 508 non-uniformed); 70.0% male / 30.0% female; staff-to-prisoner ratio **1:7**; 11,630 (84.6% of uniformed staff) deployed across the **266 custodial prison units** nationally.
- Regional breakdown across **19 named regions** (see §1) shows occupancy ranging from 219.9% (Central region) to 597.7% (Kigezi region) — i.e. even the *least*-congested region was operating at over double its approved capacity that month.
- Average number of children in prison with incarcerated mothers: **270**.

**March 2025 report** ("Monthly Statistics Summary, March 2025"):
- Population: **79,333** total (Convicts **40,956 / 51.6%**; Remands **37,754 / 47.6%**; Debtors **623 / 0.8%**) — down 0.3% from February 2025's 79,574.
- **Approved capacity: 22,019** (up 3.6% from February's 21,257 — i.e. capacity itself grew that month, likely new construction/units coming online).
- **Occupancy rate: 360.3%** (down from 374.3% in February, a relative decrease attributable to the capacity increase).
- Monthly population trend (July 2024-March 2025) stayed in a narrow band, roughly 78,057 to 79,574.
- Staff: grand total **13,941** (13,449 uniformed, 492 non-uniformed); full uniformed rank table quoted in §1.

**World Prison Brief** (prisonstudies.org), an independent academic aggregator that sources its Uganda figures from Uganda Prisons Service data, reports (as of its most recently fetched profile in this research, dated July 2026): total prison population **83,126**; **270** institutions; official capacity **23,437**, giving an occupancy rate of **354.7%**; and a **pre-trial/remand population share of 45.8%**. **[Secondary, but UPS-sourced]** Read together with the two UPS reports above, the remand share appears to be on a slow downward trend across these three snapshots: 47.9% (Jan 2024) → 47.6% (Mar 2025) → 45.8% (per WPB's most recent aggregation) — cited with each figure's own date rather than treated as one static number.

**Congestion detail (August 2025):** a web-search synthesis of UPS's own Monthly Statistical Abstract for August 2025 (not independently re-fetched and gs-extracted in full in this research pass, so treated as secondary-confidence despite the ultimate source being UPS itself) reports: **24 prison units (8.9% of total units) at over 600% occupancy**; **148 units (55.0%) "very congested"**; only **25 units (9.3%) below 200% occupancy**. **[Secondary — search-engine synthesis of a UPS primary document]**

**Older, dated context (FY2016/17), from a Ministry of Finance Ministerial Policy Statement for Vote 145 Uganda Prisons** (a government budget document reporting UPS-supplied achievement data, gs-extracted and read in full): daily average population **49,940** (up 8.8% from 45,920); occupancy **300.6%** (up from 276.4%); escape rate reduced from 8.4 to **8 per 1,000** held prisoners; mortality rate maintained at **0.75 per 1,000** prisoners; recidivism reduced from 21% to 20%; **average length of stay on remand: 18.7 months for capital offenders, 2.5 months for petty offenders**; a **daily average of 1,099 prisoners produced to 213 courts** nationwide; remand population share reduced from 54.6% to 51.6% that year. **[Primary — government document, but the data itself is nearly a decade old and must not be presented as current]**

**Bottom line for design purposes:** UPS runs and publishes a live, granular, monthly, region-by-region statistical reporting operation on its own public website — this is a much richer and more current public-statistics practice than was found for ODPP (whose sibling research could not locate an equivalent published annual report). A Pleadli Prisons product could reasonably assume population/congestion/staffing reporting is already a well-established internal UPS discipline (monthly cadence, by region, by rank) rather than something that would need to be invented from scratch.
